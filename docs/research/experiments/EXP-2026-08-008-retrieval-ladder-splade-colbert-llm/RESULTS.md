# EXP-2026-08-008 — Results

**Collection:** LongEval-Sci (CLEF), `longeval-sci-2026/snapshot-1/train`.
**Qrels:** `dctr` — click-model–derived pseudo-relevance, **not** human assessment.
**Scope:** `pooled`, 84,489 documents — a **re-ranking** evaluation. `R@100` is a
within-scope diagnostic and is not a recall claim about the production corpus.
**Queries:** 100. **Runs:** 1. No confidence intervals — see §6 and ISSUES.md §7.

The table is generated by `code/collect.py` from `eval/results/*.json` and lives
at [`tables/ladder.md`](tables/ladder.md). Do not edit it by hand.

> **SUPERSEDED IN PART by [`EXP-2026-08-009`](../EXP-2026-08-009-crossencoder-paired-intervals/RESULTS.md).**

> intervals. The six that were later re-tested **all span zero**, including the two
> headline results below — the cross-encoder beating BM25 (+0.0088, sign p = 0.91)
> and beating its own fusion base (+0.0243, sign p = 0.25). On `sci-2025-01`'s 492

> and the recommendation in §7 to adopt a cross-encoder is withdrawn.** What still
> stands is what was never a small effect: SPLADE's deficit as a retriever, the
> LLM's order gap, ColBERT's deficit, and the recall gaps — the last of which are

## 1. The headline

`EXP-2026-08-001` established that nothing in this repository beat plain BM25 on
this collection, which is why C-010 stands refuted. **A cross-encoder reranker is
the first stage that does.**

| | nDCG@10 | nDCG@100 | AP | R@100 | P@10 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| `fusion_dense_pool` — **what ships** | 0.2937 | 0.3829 | 0.2360 | 0.5988 | 0.2010 |
| `bm25_only` — the bar from EXP-001 | 0.3212 | 0.4314 | 0.2760 | 0.6903 | 0.2100 |
| `xenc_rerank` p500 d250 — **best arm** | **0.3307** | **0.4504** | 0.2780 | **0.7199** | **0.2150** |

Against what ships: **+12.6% nDCG@10 and +20.2% R@100**. Against BM25 alone:
**+3.0% nDCG@10 and +4.3% R@100**, winning on every metric in the table
simultaneously rather than trading one for another.

Read the second row carefully before celebrating. Beating BM25 by 0.0095 nDCG@10
at n = 100 with no interval computed is *suggestive*, not settled — and this
record has twice before failed to separate a small effect from no effect (Q-011,
Q-013). The margin against the *deployed* configuration is four times larger and
correspondingly safer.

## 2. Rung by rung

### SPLADE — the pre-registered hypothesis is falsified, but the story is not "useless"

| Arm | nDCG@10 | R@100 |
|---|---:|---:|
| `bm25_only` | 0.3212 | 0.6903 |
| `splade_only` | **0.2617** | 0.6173 |
| `fusion_bm25_dense_splade` | 0.3184 | **0.7141** |

The `[PRE]` hypothesis said SPLADE beats BM25 and named the falsifier explicitly.
It fired: SPLADE alone is 18.5% *worse* than BM25 on nDCG@10. The reason recorded
in advance is the likely one — SPLADE++ is trained on MS MARCO web queries and
this is scientific titles and abstracts, and sparse neural retrievers are known to
generalize worse than BM25 off their training distribution. Note the company it
keeps: the organizers' own 4B-parameter dense baseline also loses to BM25 here.

**But as a third RRF channel it produces the highest recall on the ladder** —
R@100 0.7141, above BM25's 0.6903 and well above the two-channel union pool's
0.6511 — at essentially no nDCG@10 cost against the fusion baseline it extends
(0.3184 vs 0.3056). And that wider pool is what the best arm reranks.

So the precise finding is: **SPLADE is a poor retriever on this collection and a
good recall contributor to a fusion over it.** Those are different claims and the
falsification only touches the first.

### ColBERT — falsified, and it is the one rung that costs you

| Arm | nDCG@10 | vs. its base |
|---|---:|---:|
| `fusion_union_pool` p100 (base) | 0.3056 | — |
| `colbert_rerank` d50 | 0.2951 | **−0.0105** |
| `colbert_rerank` d100 | 0.2903 | **−0.0153** |
| `fusion_union_pool` p500 (base) | 0.3015 | — |
| `colbert_rerank` p500 d250 | 0.2919 | **−0.0096** |
| `colbert_rerank` p500 d1000 | 0.2919 | **−0.0096** |
| `cascade` ColBERT-250 → xenc-50 | 0.3248 | vs. 0.3304 for the cross-encoder alone |

Late-interaction reranking makes the fusion ranking **worse** — on both bases, at
every depth tested, and monotonically with depth on the shallow one — and
inserting it ahead of the cross-encoder degrades the cross-encoder too. The
`[PRE]` hypothesis is falsified. The one thing it does not damage is recall
(R@100 0.7121 against its base's 0.7098), which is consistent with it shuffling
relevant documents around inside the pool rather than discarding them.

This one deserves a caveat the others do not, and it is recorded rather than
buried: the implementation was not independently validated against a published
ColBERT benchmark number. `answerai-colbert-small-v1` is used through
`fastembed`'s `query_embed`/`embed` asymmetry, and document vectors are stored at
float16. Either could in principle degrade MaxSim. **"ColBERT as implemented here
hurts" is measured; "late interaction does not work on this collection" is not.**

### Cross-encoder — the one that works, and its depth curve

| Base | d50 | d100 | d250 | d1000 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| `fusion_union_pool` p100 (59–97 candidates) | 0.3299 | 0.3304 | 0.3304 | — |
| `fusion_union_pool` p500 (151–290 candidates) | — | 0.3267 | **0.3307** | 0.3306 |
| `fusion_bm25_dense_splade` p100 (130–206) | — | 0.3305 | — | — |

Two things to read off this.

**Depth pays, but only once there is depth to use.** On the shallow base, d100 and
d250 are byte-identical because the base only ever emits 59–97 candidates — the
sweep was vacuous, which is a finding about production, not a bug (see §3). On the
deep base the curve is real: 0.3267 → 0.3307 → 0.3306. Reranking past 100 buys
+0.004 and **saturates by 250**. That is a much smaller effect than the general
advice "push rerank depth from 20 to 100+" would suggest, and it is now measured
rather than assumed.

**Recall feeds precision.** The two best arms are the two with the widest pools:
the SPLADE three-channel base (R@100 0.7141 → nDCG@10 0.3305) and the deep union
pool (0.7199 → 0.3307). A reranker cannot promote what retrieval never surfaced,
and the numbers behave accordingly.

### The LLM rung — position bias is real, large, and now measured

Every arm below reranks the identical candidate set. `R@100` is identical across
all three (0.6511) because none of them changes what was retrieved.

| Arm | nDCG@10 | nDCG@100 | AP | RR |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| `fusion_union_pool` (base) | 0.3056 | 0.4129 | 0.2562 | 0.4686 |
| `llm_rerank` — listwise, base order | 0.3133 | 0.4304 | 0.2640 | **0.5320** |
| `llm_rerank_reversed` — **same call, reversed input** | **0.2563** | 0.3734 | 0.2066 | 0.3837 |
| `llm_rubric` — pointwise, order-free | 0.3099 | 0.4175 | 0.2606 | 0.4794 |

**Reversing the presentation order costs 0.0570 nDCG@10 — an 18.2% relative
drop — with the candidate set held identical.** This is the cleanest result in the
experiment: there is no confound, because nothing changed except the order the
model read the list in.

**Correction to a first reading.** The obvious interpretation — "the model just
copies the order it was given" — is **wrong**, and `code/llm_diagnosis.py` was
written to test it. The full diagnosis is
[`tables/llm_diagnosis.md`](tables/llm_diagnosis.md); the load-bearing numbers:

| Measurement | Value |
|---|---:|
| Kendall tau, forward arm's output vs the order it was shown | +0.380 |
| Kendall tau, reversed arm's output vs the order it was shown | **−0.072** |
| Positions changed inside the 20-item window, forward arm | **15.97 / 20** |
| Top-10 overlap, forward vs reversed | 0.72 |
| nDCG@10 if the window were copied verbatim from the reversed input | **0.1064** |

The model reorders almost everything it is shown — 16 of 20 positions — and the
reversed arm's output is essentially **uncorrelated with its own input**
(τ = −0.072), not aligned with it. Had it copied, it would have scored 0.1064; it
scored 0.2561. So it is not an order-copier.

What it is, is **unstable**. The same candidate set produces a top-10 that is only
72% the same depending on presentation order, so **28% of what reaches the top ten
is decided by the order the model happened to read the list in.** And re-running
the forward arm at temperature 0 moved it from 0.3133 to 0.3177 — a ±0.004 wobble
against a claimed gain over the base of +0.008 to +0.013, while the reversed arm
reproduced to 0.0002 and the rubric to 0.0004. The instability is specific to
listwise reranking.

Three consequences follow.

1. **The listwise arm is not adding signal.** Its per-query record against the
   base is **29 better, 31 worse, 40 tied** — a coin flip. The positive aggregate
   comes from the size of a few wins, not from being right more often. Combined
   with the run-to-run wobble, there is no basis here for saying it improves the
   ranking.
2. **Its apparent gain is anchoring, not judgment.** Handed a good order it lands
   near a good answer; handed a bad one it lands well below the base it was asked
   to improve. A method whose output quality tracks its input's quality that
   strongly is inheriting, not evaluating — even though, mechanically, it is not
   copying.
3. **The rubric looks better than it is.** `llm_rubric` scores 0.3095 with a
   17/7/76 win/loss/tie record — but τ = +0.820 against the base order, because
   it uses on average **1.99 distinct values out of 4** across a 20-candidate
   window. Nearly everything ties, and ties fall back to the fusion rank. The
   rubric arm is mostly the base ranking showing through.

### Can the model judge relevance at all?

This is the question the deployed percent-match rests on, and the rubric's raw
verdicts answer it directly:

| Judged grade | mean rubric score (0–3) | n |
|---|---:|---:|
| 0 (unjudged or irrelevant) | 2.423 | 1,689 |
| 1 | 2.818 | 137 |
| 2 | 2.759 | 174 |

**The scale is not monotonic in relevance**: grade 2 — the most relevant documents
in the collection — scores *below* grade 1. And almost everything lands in a
narrow band near the top of the scale.

Read the grade-0 row carefully: in a pooled collection an unjudged document near
the top of a strong ranking is frequently relevant-but-never-assessed, so that
bucket understates the model and the 2.42-vs-2.8 gap is a **lower bound** on
discrimination. The grade-1-versus-grade-2 comparison is the clean one, because
both are judged — and it shows no ordering at all.

Two caveats that must travel with this. The rubric is **0–3 by this arm's design**,
while production emits **0–100**; a finer scale might discriminate where a
four-point one collapses (Q-020). And `dctr` grades are click-derived, so the
1-versus-2 distinction is itself noisy.

What survives both caveats is the part that needs no qrels at all: **the model
used two distinct values to describe twenty candidates.** That is a property of
its output alone, and it is the reason the rubric cannot order a feed.

This is the first evidence in the record on audit item W11 (order-robustness for
the LLM scorer) and the first real evidence bearing on C-004's calibration half.
It does not close W11 — that item is about the *deployed* scorer, and production
does not rank at all (ISSUES.md §6) — but it removes the excuse
that the question is untestable.

## 3. An incidental finding about production

The deployed fusion emits far fewer candidates than anything downstream assumes.
Measured from the run files, `fusion_union_pool` at `pool_per_interest = 100`
returns **59–97 candidates per query, median 74**, from a pool of up to 200. The
cause is `fuse_interest`'s `DEFAULT_INTEREST_FLOOR = 0.15`, applied after min-max
normalization: it discards more than half the pool.

Two consequences, both config-level and neither requiring a code change to test
further:

- **It caps rerank depth.** Nothing can rerank 250 candidates that were never
  emitted. Any advice to "rerank deeper" is inert until this is changed.
- **It caps recall.** Widening to `pool_per_interest = 500` lifts R@100 from
  0.6511 to 0.7098 on the same fusion code, and to 0.7199 after reranking.

This is a measurement, not a recommendation. It bears directly on **Q-006** (where
deepening the pool stops substituting for widening it) and on **Q-012** (whether
the deployed threshold or the surfacing backstop is the binding cut), and it is
scoped to LongEval — **Q-001** applies unchanged.

## 4. Cost

Everything below is one-time and cached content-addressed; a re-run of the whole
ladder loads from disk. No corpus was embedded twice, and the dense channel was
never embedded at all — the 84,489-row matrix was **row-sliced out of the cached
869,902-row full-corpus matrix**, which is why step 1 of the protocol costs
minutes rather than the ~37 minutes a fresh encode would have.

| Item | Cost |
|---|---|
| Freeze subset + slice dense matrix | 369 s, once |
| SPLADE encode (84,489 docs) | 3,451 s (~30 docs/s) |
| ColBERT encode (84,489 docs → 22.4M token vectors) | 3,640 s (~26 docs/s) |
| Every scored arm | 8–913 s each |
| Total | ~4.5 h wall clock, on CPU, no GPU |

Two operational notes worth carrying: running both encoders concurrently was
**catastrophically** slower than serially (a shard projected at 3 minutes took 20;
ISSUES.md §5), and `fastembed`'s process sharding is what makes a 32-core host
usable for this at all.

## 5. What this changes in the claims ledger

- **C-008 (union pool costs recall) — reinforced.** Every arm here that widened
  the pool gained recall, and the two best arms are the two widest.
- **C-012 (late-interaction reranking beats the fusion ranking) — refuted**, with
  the implementation caveat in §2 attached to the refutation.
- **C-013 (a cross-encoder over the fusion ranking beats BM25 alone) —
  supported**, on this collection, with the small-margin caveat in §1.
- **C-014 (SPLADE as a third RRF channel raises recall over two-channel fusion) —
  supported**, on this collection.
- **C-015 (an LLM listwise reranker's output is substantially determined by
  presentation order) — supported**, and this is the strongest single result here.
- **C-010 (the deployed stack beats a strong lexical baseline) stays refuted.**
  Nothing in the *deployed* configuration moved. What beats BM25 is a stage that
  is not deployed.

## 6. Threats to validity

1. **n = 100, one run, no intervals.** The margins this experiment leans on — the
   SPLADE deficit (0.060), the LLM order gap (0.057), the ColBERT deficit
   (0.011–0.015) — are large. The margin against BM25 (0.0095) is not, and should
   not be quoted as settled.
2. **Click-derived qrels**, not human assessment.
3. **`pooled` scope.** A re-ranking evaluation. Recall does not transfer.
4. **The task is not Mycelium's task.** LongEval is `query → documents`; the
   product is `declared-interest profile → feed`. `docs/evaluation.md`'s claims
   boundary applies without modification, and nothing here licenses a production
   change on its own.
5. **The ColBERT implementation is unvalidated** against an external benchmark
   number (§2).
6. **The LLM's identity is not pinned** — `skynet` is a relay alias whose upstream
   may change between runs, and production runs at temperature 0.2 while these
   arms ran at 0. Audit item W1 remains open and this experiment is affected by it.
7. **Protocol partially written after the fact** (ISSUES.md §1). The SPLADE,
   ColBERT, and depth hypotheses are genuine pre-registrations; the cross-encoder
   and LLM ones are not.

## 7. Which stages are worth their cost

The ladder table asks "is this arm good". That is not the same question as "should
this stage be added", because arms differ from each other by more than one thing.
[`tables/marginal.md`](tables/marginal.md) is a **chain** — each row is the row
above plus exactly one stage — so a delta there is that stage's own contribution.

Three stages each contribute about the same, and they **stack almost additively**:
+0.0119, +0.0128, +0.0121 on nDCG@10, taking the production shape from 0.2937 to
0.3305. None is redundant with the others.

**The cheapest two get almost all of it.** Union pool plus a cross-encoder at
depth 50 reaches 0.3299 — **98% of the total measured gain** — with *no corpus
encoding of any kind*. A cross-encoder never builds an index; it scores query and
document together at query time, so its only cost is per-query latency.

**Depth 50 is the operating point, not 250.** 0.3299 at depth 50 against 0.3306 at
depth 1000 is +0.0007 for 3.7× the wall clock, and 0.0007 is far inside the noise
this design can resolve. The earlier framing of "best arm = p500 d250" is
technically true and operationally misleading: that arm buys nothing over depth 50
that this experiment can measure.

**SPLADE's contribution changes character once a reranker is present.** Before
reranking it is worth +0.0128 nDCG@10. *After* — comparing the two depth-100
cross-encoder arms, which differ only in whether SPLADE fed the pool — nDCG@10 is
identical (0.3304 vs 0.3305) while nDCG@100 rises +0.0172 and R@100 +0.0630. So
once a cross-encoder is sorting the head, SPLADE stops buying top-10 precision and
buys **depth and recall** instead. Whether that is worth 58 minutes of encoding per
84k documents plus a second index to maintain depends entirely on whether the
deliverable is a handful of items or a longer slate — for a 25-item slate,
nDCG@100 is nearer the right metric than nDCG@10, and the case is stronger.

**Two stages cost and do not pay.** ColBERT is negative at every depth and costs
3,640 s of encoding plus 22.4M token vectors. The LLM as a ranker is inside its own
rerun noise with a 29/31/40 per-query record (§2), and costs a model call per query
listwise or twenty per query pointwise.

## 8. What to do next, in order

1. **Q-001 on the production corpus.** Every result here is on LongEval. The
   cross-encoder gain and the interest-floor finding both need checking against
   real declared-interest queries before either drives a change.
2. **Paired bootstrap intervals** on the three comparisons that matter, at a
   larger query set (`sci-2025-01` has 492). This is cheap and it is the difference
   between "the cross-encoder beats BM25" and "the cross-encoder may beat BM25".
3. **Validate the ColBERT implementation** against a published benchmark number
   before letting §2's refutation stand unqualified.
4. **The rubric arm is the interesting product direction**, not the listwise one —
   same quality, order-free, fully parseable, and it emits a per-candidate score
   that a UI could show. That is much closer to what the deployed annotator
   already does than a reranker is.
