### Evidence #1 [paper] Assembling wild berry transitions: The spatial work of projectified sustainability transitions in Västerbotten, Sweden
Authors: Nils Pettersson, Linn Axelsson
Venue: Geoforum
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.geoforum.2026.104809
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
In Europe, widespread projectification has led to sustainability transitions increasingly being enacted through projects. While projects have been seen as spaces in which multiple actors from different levels of society can interact to accelerate transitions, this paper examines the deeper implications for transition work that arise from relying on the project form. Drawing on an assemblage-topological lens, it analyses regional transition projects in northern Europe to explore the spatial work of project-making, showing how heterogeneous elements are drawn into relational proximity and held there, as well as how relations are forged across dispersed initiatives. The paper examines transition projects centred around wild berries in Västerbotten, Sweden. Through an analysis of project documents and interviews with project and case leaders, as well as related actors, it reveals projectified transitions to be dynamic and contested processes whose effects emerge from the spatial labour that continually shapes the scope and composition of transition projects. Furthermore, it shows that transitions are not driven by projects per se, but also by the relational practices through which actors pool resources and mobilise connections across multiple project configurations to enact transitions despite fragmentation. By tracing how transitions emerge from the spatial work required to assemble relations that operate within, across, between, and despite projects, this paper shows that transition work does not unfold through the mere execution of project plans, but rather through ongoing efforts to navigate the affordances and constraints of a projectified funding landscape.

Authors: Reinhard Wagner, Mladen Radujković
Venue: Proceedings of the European Academy of Sciences and Arts
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.4081/peasa.89

Abstract:
Europe is confronted with technological, economic, environmental, geopolitical and societal challenges that demand more than policy intentions and established organisational routines. They demand the capacity to turn ideas into action and ambitions into tangible change. Projects have become one of the principal organisational forms through which such change is realised. This Academic Engagement/Perspective paper reflects on projectification from a European perspective and distinguishes three interrelated arenas: the private economy, where projects enable innovation, transformation and competitiveness; the public sector, where projects translate policies into infrastructure, reforms and public value; and society at large, where projects provide vehicles for civic engagement, arts, culture and collective action. Projectification is understood here as the increasing prevalence and societal embeddedness of projects and project practices. We distinguish it from project capability, meaning the ability to select, govern, resource, deliver and learn from projects. Evidence from Germany, Norway, Iceland, Croatia and Italy shows that projectification is substantial but not uniform. We therefore argue that projectification should be understood as a contextually embedded societal transformation rather than as a universal model. Europe does not simply need more projects; it needs project capabilities suited to different institutional settings and able to connect temporary initiatives with longer-term organisational and societal purposes.

### Evidence #7 [paper] Predicting changes in perceived project success across lifecycle stages: A respondent-grouped hierarchical machine learning approach
Authors: Udechukwu Ojiako
Venue: Zenodo (CERN European Organization for Nuclear Research)
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23372591
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Judgements of whether a project will succeed are not fixed: practitioners may revise their assessments when they consider different lifecycle stages. Using a cross-sectional survey of 1,265 practitioners who gave initial and follow-up success ratings for up to six project stages (6,184 rating pairs), this study develops and tests a two-stage machine-learning framework. The first stage predicts whether a paired rating differs; the second predicts whether the revised rating is higher or lower. Five tree ensembles were compared with regularised and stepwise logistic regression under respondent-grouped cross-validation, so that every prediction was made for respondents excluded from model fitting. About half of the paired ratings differed. Upward revisions were more common for planning and milestone stage assessments and downward revisions from the handover stage onwards. The operations stage had the highest revision rate. Change occurrence was moderately predictable, whereas direction was highly predictable once a change had occurred, although much of the direction signal came from the initial rating on a bounded scale. In leave-one-country-out validation, direction transferred across countries, but change occurrence did not do so consistently. Psychological distance offers a possible interpretation of the stage-associated patterns, but the survey did not follow projects over time or test that mechanism directly. The framework is therefore a preliminary decision-support approach, not a validated early-warning system. For this reason, further local retraining and prospective testing are required before full implementation.

Abstract:
Digital Twin (DT) technology has emerged as a potential enabler of systemic reform in the built environment industry, promising improved project delivery and long-term value creation. Yet, within a sector characterised by fragmented delivery models and adversarial procurement practices, DT integration remains limited and poorly defined. Policy direction remains underdeveloped, and governance support has diminished following the closure of initiatives such as the Centre for Digital Built Britain (CDBB), leaving a gap in national-level coordination at a critical moment for mainstream adoption. Despite rising industry interest and visibility among providers, and as the industry heads towards a more digitised, integrated future, there is a risk that DT may follow the path of earlier innovations: widely promoted but ultimately constrained by structural inertia and the absence of coherent implementation strategies. Fundamental questions persist around the distinction between DT and other digital tools, as well as how DT supports net-zero goals, and when and how it should be implemented. This study addresses these gaps by developing a novel Digital Twin–Collaborative Procurement (DT—CP) framework structured across the Royal Institute of British Architects (RIBA) Plan of Work. The framework responds to the unique demands of DT implementation, introducing a preparatory Stage 0+ focused on organisational alignment, data governance, and long-term lifecycle planning. It also proposes a new classification model for DT to reflect stage-specific functionalities and support integration across the project timeline. The resulting framework is retrospectively applied to the Edinburgh Home Demonstrator (EHD) Programme to evaluate its practical relevance within public sector housing delivery, and provide an illustrative application of its stage by stage implementation. The findings demonstrate the interdependence between CP principles and the successful deployment of DT, revealing that DT adoption requires not only technical provisions but also, and especially, cultural and procedural alignment. The research establishes that DT value varies across project stages, and it is most effectively realised when embedded from the pre-design phase through structured interorganisational alignment. It further positions DT as a strategic enabler of net-zero objectives, contingent on the effective capture and use of data. This research contributes a practice-oriented model for DT implementation, with implications for governance and policy development, and encourages longer-term thinking across project delivery. Ultimately, it aims to demystify DT and bridge the gap between academic discourse and delivery, motivating broader adoption of DT as a catalyst for systemic change.
