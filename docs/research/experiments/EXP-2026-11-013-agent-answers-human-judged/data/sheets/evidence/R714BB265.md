### Evidence #2 [paper] The Ceiling Holds: Testing AI/ML Architecture, Depth, and Training-Window Size Against an Empirically Measured Predictability Limit
Authors: Arun Ramanathan
Venue: Zenodo (CERN European Organization for Nuclear Research)
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.21696947
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
A common, mostly unstated assumption behind a lot of modern AI/ML work is that more data and a more powerful model can eventually predict almost anything. In the physical sciences this was tested and found wanting: chaos theory showed that some systems, like weather, have a hard limit on how far ahead they can be predicted no matter how good your model or how much data you feed it, because the limit lives in the system’s own dynamics, not in the model. This research program has already measured a limit like that for financial markets, directly and empirically, with no model involved in the measurement itself. This paper runs two tests of what that limit actually means for AI/ML in practice, plus a direct follow-up to the first test that closes an obvious loophole. First, we give five very different kinds of models — a plain average, an intentionally overfit decision tree, a reinforcement-learning agent, a generative adversarial network, and a variational autoencoder — the exact same fair amount of training data, sized to stay safely within that measured limit, and simply watch which one predicts real subsequent prices best. Since the fancier three of those five were kept deliberately simple — no hidden layers, nothing a machine learning engineer would call “deep” — we then test the obvious objection directly: we give the reinforcement-learning agent, the GAN, and the variational autoencoder one genuine hidden layer each, real nonlinearity rather than just a different training recipe, while keeping the exact same tiny amount of training data, and check whether that changes anything. Second, we take the original five models and start feeding them progressively more training data, extending further and further past that limit, the way a real team chasing “more data” often does, and watch what happens. In the first test, nothing beats the plain average; two of the fancier models essentially become the plain average. Giving those same two models a real hidden layer doesn’t change that either — they still just become the plain average, and the variational autoencoder is still unstable rather than skillful, whether linear or given a hidden layer. In the second test, every model — regardless of how it works internally — starts drifting away from reality once its training data reaches well past the measured limit, and the drift gets worse the further past it they go. This is the same pattern atmospheric scientists saw decades ago: an early, real-model-based estimate of a predictability limit, a theoretical account soon after of why it should exist, and then decades of dramatically better forecasting systems that kept running into the same wall anyway, no matter how much they improved in the meantime. We show this mainly as plots of predicted price against real price, and back the training-data test up with a plain, direct measurement of how large the errors actually get, as a percentage of price anyone can judge for themselves — not a statistical test score, not a p-value, just a straightforward number.

Abstract:
Financial forecasting under uncertainty is fundamental to strategic investment decision making. Among the factors that influence investment decisions, market efficiency plays a critical role because it determines whether historical information can provide predictive value for future market behavior. The Hurst exponent is widely used to measure persistence and weak-form market efficiency, where values close to 0.5 indicate random behavior and values above or below 0.5 indicate persistent or mean-reverting dynamics. Despite extensive research using the Hurst exponent to characterize market persistence and market-efficiency-related behavior, limited studies have investigated whether future Hurst exponent estimates can be forecast using machine learning techniques and whether these models can outperform a simple constant benchmark. This study addresses this gap by developing a machine learning framework to forecast the next-month Hurst exponent using firm-level descriptive, financial, technical, and statistical indicators and comparing their performance against a constant benchmark. A dataset comprising 141 Saudi-listed companies, 705 firm-month observations, approximately 147 predictor variables, and five monthly periods was constructed. Monthly Hurst exponent values were estimated using the Rescaled Range (R/S) method and used as the target variable. Linear Regression, Ridge Regression, Elastic Net, Support Vector Regression (SVR), Random Forest, and XGBoost were evaluated using an expanding-window time-series validation strategy with two chronological forecast origins. Model performance was assessed using Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and the coefficient of determination (R2). None of the evaluated machine learning models outperformed the constant benchmark, which achieved the lowest RMSE (0.227) and MAE (0.179). Ridge Regression performed relatively best among the machine learning models, with an RMSE of 0.236 and an MAE of 0.187, but it still failed to improve upon the benchmark, and all evaluated machine learning models produced negative R2 values. These results indicate that the selected firm-level descriptive, financial, technical, and statistical indicators provided limited incremental forecasting value relative to a simple constant benchmark within the adopted empirical design. Given the five-month observation period and limited number of chronological forecast origins, the findings are best interpreted as exploratory evidence regarding the ability of machine learning models to outperform a constant benchmark in forecasting next-month Hurst estimates, rather than as definitive evidence concerning market efficiency or the general predictability of financial markets. The study provides an empirical benchmark for future investigations into forecasting Hurst exponent estimates and changes in market persistence.
