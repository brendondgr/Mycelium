# RA3713848

## Question

Is there anything in our feed about how marine litter is transported or beached?

## Answer

Yes, the provided evidence contains multiple studies detailing the mechanisms, modeling, and observation of how marine litter is transported and beached.

Several studies utilize Lagrangian particle-tracking models to simulate the transport and coastal deposition of floating debris. For instance, a study on the Mediterranean Sea presents a data-driven stochastic Lagrangian framework that uses monthly gridded observations from shipboard surveys as initial conditions to predict transport and coastal deposition #2. Similarly, research on the coastline of Côte d'Ivoire employed a Lagrangian particle-tracking model forced by high-resolution ocean circulation data to assess seasonal beaching dynamics, finding that regional hydrodynamics play a dominant role in shaping debris distribution #5. Another study focused on the East/Japan Sea evaluated near-surface forcing combinations, such as geostrophic currents, wind-driven currents, and wave-induced drift, to improve the accuracy of floating debris transport modeling #20.

Field-based observations and specific physical mechanisms are also documented in the feed. A campaign in the Dutch Wadden Sea tracked shallow surface drifters to characterize the transport and coastal interaction dynamics of floating macroplastic in intertidal regions, noting that wind towards the coast increases the frequency of interactions #6. Additionally, a laboratory investigation explored the mechanism of particle pickup in wave–swash interactions, finding that the interaction between consecutive waves produces the vertical forces required to suspend negatively buoyant debris on natural beaches #16.

The evidence also includes specific tools and guidelines related to these processes. A code repository was shared for a tide-resolving beaching kernel developed for particle-tracking experiments, which was applied to field records from Uido, Republic of Korea #15. Furthermore, guidelines for marine monitoring and assessment of beach litter were updated by the OSPAR Beach Litter Expert Group #4. While some studies focus on the resulting accumulation rather than the transport process itself, such as those on the Norwegian coast #19, Trindade Island #1, and tropical small islands #8, the feed explicitly covers the dynamics of how litter moves to and settles on beaches.

Venue: Thalassas An International Journal of Marine Sciences

Problem (Mycelium's reading): Characterizing the characteristics and spatial distribution of marine litter on Trindade Island, a remote South Atlantic island, to establish baselines for monitoring before the establishment of marine protected areas.
Method (Mycelium's reading): Systematic sampling of seven beaches with varying exposure to wind and substrate; quantification of litter items, categorization by purpose, and calculation of pollution indices (Clean Coast Index and Plastic Abundance Index).
Setting (Mycelium's reading): Seven beaches on Trindade Island, South Atlantic Ocean, 1200 km off the South American mainland; characterized by coarse, large pebble substrates.
Abstract:
Abstract Marine plastic litter is a major environmental problem across all oceans, including remote regions. Here, we described the characteristics and spatial distribution of marine litter stranded on the beaches of Trindade Island, in the South Atlantic Ocean, 1200 km off the South American mainland, before the establishment of marine protected areas and the initiation of a long-term research program on the island. Seven beaches differing in exposure to wind and substrate were sampled. A total of 1317 litter items were recorded, with plastics as the main litter (81.9%), mostly hard plastic fragments. Although Lixo Beach had the smallest surveyed area (425 m 2 ), it exhibited the highest litter density (0.74 items/m 2 ) and highest litter-covered area (2.2% of the sampled beach area). Domestic and household purpose items composed the bulk of identifiable items (28.9%), followed by naval (22.5%) and fishery (17.7%) pieces. Litter distribution among beaches differed, with leeward beaches accumulating significantly more and larger litter items than windward beaches, revealing substantial local-scale variation patterns in debris retention. These beaches are characterized by coarse, large pebble substrates rather than sand. Our analysis of pollution indices demonstrated that the Clean Coast Index ( CCI ) and the Plastic Abundance Index ( PAI ) are strongly correlated and suitable for long-term monitoring. This study confirms that oceanic islands are prone to accumulating marine litter, while contrasting results from other studies on Trindade Island emphasize the need for simple yet standardized protocols to support long-term monitoring of plastic pollution in remote environments.

### Evidence #2 [paper] Marine litter in the Mediterranean: observation-based Lagrangian particle tracking

Method (Mycelium's reading): A data-driven stochastic Lagrangian framework using Parcels, initialized with monthly gridded FMML observations from shipboard surveys and forced by Copernicus Marine Service Mediterranean reanalysis currents and ERA5 wind fields with a 1% windage coefficient and a 2D random-walk turbulent diffusio…
Setting (Mycelium's reading): Mediterranean Sea, specifically areas surveyed via the LIFE Conceptu Maris Project using ferries from August 2023 to December 2024.
Abstract:
Introduction The Mediterranean is a major hotspot for floating marine macro-litter (FMML) accumulation, yet spatially explicit, observation-grounded predictions of its transport and coastal deposition remain scarce. We present a data-driven stochastic Lagrangian framework that uses monthly gridded FMML observations from systematic shipboard surveys as simulation initial conditions complementing existing source-based modelling approaches. Methods Observations were collected from August 2023 to December 2024 within the LIFE Conceptu Maris Project using ferries as monitoring platforms under a standardised protocol. A total of 158 surveys detected 4,801 FMML items across approximately 6,200 km² of surveyed sea surface, of which more than 62% were artificial polymers. Observed monthly FMML item counts at 5 × 5 km resolution initialised ensemble Lagrangian simulations (18 members per target month; 17.2–32.5 million virtual particles per seeding window, 5,000 particles per observed item) using Parcels, forced by Copernicus Marine Service Mediterranean reanalysis currents and ERA5 wind fields with a 1% windage coefficient and a 2D random-walk turbulent diffusion scheme. Monthly frequency-of-occurrence maps were computed at the sea surface and on coastlines. Results The Southern Tyrrhenian and Northern Ligurian seas, including the Pelagos Sanctuary, exhibited in 2024 near-persistent high sea surface FMML occurrence, peaking in late summer–autumn and reaching a seasonal minimum in winter–early spring. The Balearic Sea showed elevated occurrence from late spring through summer (May–September), declining through autumn. The Adriatic displayed intense sub-basin-scale spreading from January to June and again in December, with summer leakage into the Northern Ionian Sea. Coastal deposition frequencies were highest on Corsica, western Sardinia and northern Tunisia, then the south-eastern Tyrrhenian and Ligurian–Tuscan margins, with Valencia–Barcelona largely confined to summer. Integration with loggerhead turtle nesting records illustrates how coastal frequency-of-occurrence maps can support conservation applications. Discussion A web-based Particle Analysis Dashboard translates these outputs into an interactive tool with Marine Protected and Conserved Area overlays for scientists and decision-makers. The framework provides a scalable, observation-anchored foundation for MSFD Descriptor 10 monitoring, transferable to other semi-enclosed sea basins. Priority future work includes complementary validation against independent beach-litter monitoring data, integration of land-based source inventories, and linkage with species distribution models.

### Evidence #3 [paper] Contrasting macro- and meso-litter accumulation in managed and unmanaged beach sectors of the northwestern Adriatic Sea
Authors: Juan Pablo Passetti, Marco Varricchione, Maria Carla de Francesco, María Laura Carranza, Antonella Penna, Silvia Casabianca
Venue: Environmental Monitoring and Assessment
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10661-026-16011-8
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Beach management practices are widely implemented in coastal environments, yet their association with litter size structure remains poorly understood. We evaluated macro- and meso-litter across vegetated (unmanaged) and non-vegetated (managed) beach areas along the northwestern Adriatic coast (Italy) through seasonal stratified surveys comprising 448 plot-level sampling events across 112 plots. Meso-litter accounted for approximately 84% of all recorded items. Negative binomial generalized linear mixed models revealed a significant interaction between litter size class and area type (p < 0.001). Macro-litter abundance was 53% lower in non-vegetated areas (p < 0.001), whereas meso-litter abundance did not differ significantly between area types (p = 0.539). Binomial mixed models further indicated a higher predicted proportion of meso-litter within the combined meso- plus macro-litter total in non-vegetated areas (OR = 2.70, p < 0.001), revealing a compositional shift toward smaller debris fractions in managed beach sectors. These findings suggest that beach management is associated with changes in litter size structure and that monitoring approaches based exclusively on macro-litter may not fully capture variation in coastal litter composition. Incorporating meso-litter into monitoring programs could therefore improve assessments of coastal litter dynamics and support more comprehensive management strategies.

### Evidence #5 [paper] Modeling dispersal and beaching of urban plastic waste along the coastline of Côte d'Ivoire.
Authors: Amemou, Hilaire, Cecchi, Philippe, Koné, Vamara, Penven, Pierrick, Lett, Christophe
Venue: Marine pollution bulletin
Year: 2026
Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42735662/
Still open/current: yes
Problem (Mycelium's reading): The processes governing the fate, dispersal, and coastal retention of urban plastic debris along the coastline of Côte d'Ivoire, which is vulnerable due to dense coastal populations and significant plastic inputs.
Method (Mycelium's reading): A Lagrangian particle-tracking model (Ichthyop) forced by surface currents from a high-resolution ocean circulation model (CROCO) to simulate the dispersal and beaching of plastic particles.
Setting (Mycelium's reading): The coastline of Côte d'Ivoire, specifically focusing on the region around Abidjan and the broader Ivorian coast.
Abstract:
Plastic pollution is a growing concern in West Africa's marine environment, yet the processes governing its fate remain poorly understood. The coastline of Côte d'Ivoire is particularly vulnerable due to dense coastal populations and significant plastic inputs from rivers and lagoons. This study investigates the dispersal and coastal retention of urban plastic debris using a Lagrangian particle-tracking model (Ichthyop) forced by surface currents from a high-resolution ocean circulation model (CROCO). We provide the first regional-scale assessment of seasonal beaching dynamics along the Ivorian coast using a high-resolution coupled hydrodynamic-Lagrangian framework. Simulations show that while a large fraction of particles is transported offshore (~52%), a substantial proportion (~48%) remains nearshore and beaches at least once. Persistent accumulation hotspots are identified around Abidjan, regardless of the release location. Seasonal variability strongly controls debris pathways. During the minor upwelling season (January-April), beaching occurs predominantly west of Abidjan, associated with weaker currents and enhanced coastal retention. In contrast, during the major upwelling season (June-September), intensified currents and mesoscale activity promote wider dispersal and shift beaching eastward. From October to December, beaching intensifies along the eastern coastline. These results highlight the dominant role of regional hydrodynamics in shaping marine debris distribution and demonstrate the value of high-resolution Lagrangian modeling for identifying pollution hotspots and supporting mitigation strategies in data-scarce coastal regions.

### Evidence #6 [paper] Groundings of drifters in the Wadden Sea inform the transport of floating macroplastic
Authors: Marc Schneiter, Rolf Hut, Erik van Sebille
Venue: Ocean science
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.5194/os-22-3595-2026
Still open/current: yes
Problem (Mycelium's reading): Understanding the transport and coastal interaction dynamics of floating macroplastic in intertidal regions, specifically through the analysis of drifter groundings.
Method (Mycelium's reading): Field campaign involving the release and tracking of 24 shallow surface drifters in the Dutch Wadden Sea; manual and statistical automatic detection of drifter groundings; characterization of groundings relative to wind conditions, tides, and duration.
Setting (Mycelium's reading): Dutch Wadden Sea, an intertidal region driven by semi-diurnal tides and wind.
Abstract:
Abstract. We study floating marine macro plastic pollution with a dedicated campaign where we released and tracked 24 shallow surface drifters in the Dutch Wadden Sea, an intertidal region where water dynamics are driven by semi-diurnal tides and wind. The campaign captured water surface transport patterns, and around 150 observations of the drifters interacting with the coast and mudflats, where groundings of varying durations occurred. We identify these transitions in the recorded trajectories from both manual inspection and by formulating and evaluating a statistical approach for automatic detection. We then characterize the transitions by relating them to the coinciding wind conditions and tides, and with the duration of the groundings. The results of this analysis generally agree with the intuitions that wind towards the coast increases the frequency of interactions and that the drifters are rarely re-floated during falling tides. Furthermore, we find that groundings tend to be shorter duration when they occur shorter before the next high tide. We present multiple visualizations that characterize the groundings in more detail. The results provide a statistical characterization of coastal interactions that can be integrated in models and used for the planning of beach-cleanups. They may be improved by considering the specific environmental conditions of the campaign, and by analyzing the influence of the weight and size of the drifters, which are both larger than typical floating macroplastic.

### Evidence #7 [paper] Microplastic pollution along the Ningaloo Coast World Heritage Area: Local source contributions within a remote marine protected area
Authors: Naomi Hill, Marina Santana, Renae Hovey, Cherie A. Motti, Catarina Serra-Gonçalves, Geoffrey D. Yau, Samantha Jaworski, Ronen Galaiduk
Venue: Marine Pollution Bulletin
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.marpolbul.2026.120326
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
This study provides the first quantitative assessment of microplastic (MP) contamination on beaches along the Ningaloo Coast World Heritage Area (NCWHA; Western Australia), a remote Marine Protected Area of multiple use, including tourism. Sediment collected across seven remote and urbanised beaches were processed using elutriation and density floatation separation, with putative MPs analysed by stereomicroscopy and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy. Microplastics were detected at all sites and in 88% of samples, but at relatively low levels (0- 8.5 MPs L-1) compared to many urbanised coastlines. Concentrations significantly differed amongst sites, indicating spatially heterogeneous accumulation. This was explained by proximity to boat ramps, distance to townships and beach aspect, with higher concentrations of MPs on north-west facing beaches. Polyethylene terephthalate (PET; 38.2%) and polypropylene (PP; 20.2%) dominated polymer composition, with manufactured fibres (72.4%) the most common shape across all sites. Consequently, no relationship was found between polymer or shape and site-specific predictors. Despite its remoteness and protected status, the NCWHA remains vulnerable to MP inputs, potentially from local human activities with dispersion influenced by natural processes. This baseline provides a critical foundation for future monitoring efforts and evaluation of targeted management interventions at high-use access points.

### Evidence #8 [paper] Cross-ecosystem assessment of macroplastic pollution in mangrove, coral reef, seagrass, and beach habitats of tropical small islands.
Authors: Yunanto, Agung, Wisha, Ulung Jantama, Romdon, Ahmad, Arifin, Zainal, Priyono, Bayu, Falahudin, Dede, Handayani, Tri, Ondara, Koko
Venue: Marine pollution bulletin
Year: 2026
Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42896361/

Abstract:
Marine plastic pollution poses an increasing threat to tropical coastal ecosystems; however, comparative understanding of macroplastic accumulation across interconnected habitats remains limited, particularly in small-island environments. This study assessed macroplastic density and composition across mangroves, coral reefs, seagrass meadows, and beaches on three tropical small islands (Panjang, Semujur, and Ketawai) in western Bangka, Indonesia. Field surveys were conducted in May 2025 using ecosystem-specific transect methods, and macroplastic debris was quantified in terms of abundance, weight, volume, and composition. Macroplastic accumulation exhibited clear habitat-dependent patterns. Across the three islands, beaches consistently recorded the highest debris densities (0.193 ± 0.0083-0.680 ± 0.0310 items/m2), whereas coral reefs exhibited the lowest densities (0.003 ± 0.0005-0.009 ± 0.0011 items/m2). Seagrass meadows showed generally low to moderate accumulation (0.002 ± 0.0003-0.082 ± 0.0102 items/m2), while mangroves exhibited intermediate densities (0.067 ± 0.0027 items/m2). Habitat-level differences were statistically significant (Kruskal-Wallis: χ2 = 78.9, p < 0.001), whereas total debris density did not differ significantly among islands. Food- and beverage-related plastics dominated beach and seagrass habitats, whereas coral reefs consisted exclusively of fishing-related debris. Hierarchical clustering and NMDS ordination (stress = 0.1084) consistently revealed stronger compositional structuring by habitat than by island. PERMANOVA further indicated that habitat accounted for a greater proportion of compositional variation than island identity (R2 = 0.465, p = 0.104). These findings demonstrate that macroplastic accumulation across tropical small islands is primarily structured by habitat characteristics and local retention processes, highlighting the need for habitat-specific mitigation measures and integrated coastal management.

Abstract:

Large plastic items such as bottles and bags degrade in the environment, ultimately breaking down into microplastics (MPs). Investigating the relationship between environmental conditions and plastic degradation is crucial for developing strategies to mitigate the impact of MPs pollution in freshwater ecosystems. These findings suggest that consumer packaging, especially related to food and beverages, is a major contributor to plastic waste in the Danube, while specialized products like footwear and fishing materials also play significant roles.

### Evidence #16 [paper] Pickup of Negatively Buoyant Particles in Wave–Swash Interactions
Authors: Samuel T. Salemink‐Harry, Claudio Meza‐Valle, Nimish Pujara
Venue: Water Waves
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.1007/s42286-026-00149-6
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Abstract The transport of sand, cobbles, and negatively buoyant debris in the swash zone governs their mass exchange between land and sea. To explore a candidate mechanism for the suspension of such particles on natural beaches, we conducted an idealized laboratory investigation of particle pickup on an impermeable sloping beach in a wave flume. We generated wave–swash interactions using consecutive solitary waves with varying amplitude and separation times and observed the pickup of a hollow aluminum cylinder in the swash zone constrained to vertical motion by a wire. Pickup occurred at the arrival of the second wave, and not the first, indicating that in our experiments the wave–swash interaction produced the vertical forces required for pickup. Moreover, pickup did not occur in many interactions where previous measurements had indicated large vertical flow accelerations. When pickup did occur, the separation time between waves was slightly smaller than the duration of the first wave’s swash flow. A model of particle pickup based on vertical flow accelerations over-predicted the maximum particle velocity, suggesting that large, short-lived vertical flow accelerations in wave–swash interactions can potentially be sufficient to cause pickup. Variability in the particle trajectories suggests that fluctuating (turbulent) vertical velocities at the time of pickup can enhance or dampen the pickup. Taken together, and acknowledging the idealized setup, our results are consistent with the intermittency of sediment suspension and debris pickup observed in the field being associated with the highly variable vertical flow accelerations and velocities across the zone of wave–swash interactions.

Setting (Mycelium's reading): The study focused on the south-west coast of the UK, specifically the Tamar and Plym Rivers, Plymouth Sound, and Lyme Bay, Devon. In situ sampling transects were conducted from April to October 2024, and data from 2019 was analyzed for a mussel farm in Lyme Bay.
Abstract:
Exposure to bacteria and viruses in fresh and coastal waters, whether through recreation or food, represents a potentially significant source of infectious diseases in humans. The reliance on in situ 'spot' sampling conducted at regular intervals at discharge locations and in bathing waters may not be sufficient to capture fecal contamination events. Hydrographic modelling of contamination risk from bacteria can provide high temporal and spatial simulation of particle trajectories. In this study we use in situ measurements with the Finite Volume Community Ocean Model (FVCOM) and a Lagrangian particle tracking model (PyLag) of Escherichia coli (E. coli) from point source discharge to bathing sites and shellfish farms along the south-west coast of the UK. In situ sampling transects were conducted from the Tamar and Plym Rivers and off-shore to Plymouth Sound from April to October 2024 for the analysis of E. coli cell counts. A forward Lagrangian trajectory model was used to track where the E. coli occurred from the rivers to the coast. During heavy rainfall on 11 April and 16 October 2024, and also during a dry period on 08 July 2024, significant numbers of E. coli were traced to local tourist beaches to both the east and west of Plymouth Sound. In addition, a large mussel farm in Lyme Bay, Devon was affected by elevated counts of E. coli in 2019 resulting in the downgrading of the classification of its offshore shellfish production site. We also used particle back-trajectory modelling, combined sewage overflow and river flow data to give insight into possible sources of the elevated E. coli counts at the mussel farm in May and October 2019. The backward trajectory Lagrangian particle tracking indicated that the potential pathway of E. coli contamination was from sewage discharge in local rivers. The results illustrated that rainfall and river volume alone are not sufficient to forecast trajectories of E. coli, but that surface current and direction are also necessary. The hydrographic modelling method to track fecal contamination is applicable to coastal waters and could enhance marine monitoring of these contaminants both in the UK, EU and globally.

Abstract:
Abstract Understanding the mechanisms behind coastal plastic accumulation is crucial for more targeted clean-up actions and policy-making. We used a systematically collected dataset for plastic pollution along the Norwegian coast, added physical and socio-environmental response variables to it, and used linear regression to identify the most important factors that influence plastic accumulation. We used 446 sampling transects on 170 beaches from Norway’s south-eastern, southern, western and northern coasts. Plastic weight and count were chosen as response variables in several linear models, with different explanatory variables included. Although the explained variance in plastic pollution did not lead to any meaningful insights regarding plastic distribution, other insights were gained. Measuring plastic pollution by weight or item count led to different correlations of predictor variables. There were significant regional differences in models, and some predictor variables clearly show different values depending on the region. Furthermore, geographical beaching patterns of different types of plastic debris along the Norwegian coast found in the literature were confirmed. The southern coast had the largest number of plastic items per sampling area, whilst the north displayed fewer but heavier items. Dividing the data by region seems to be desirable, but more samples per region are needed.

### Evidence #20 [paper] Data-driven enhancement of ocean surface forcing for accurate floating debris transport modelling in the East/Japan Sea
Authors: Hong Thi My Tran, Young Gyu Park, Jun Myoung Choi
Venue: Ocean science
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.5194/os-22-2533-2026
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Abstract. Floating debris transport in marginal seas is controlled by a combination of geostrophic currents, wind-driven currents, wave-induced drift, and direct wind forcing, but the relative importance of these processes remains difficult to quantify without in-situ trajectory observations. This study evaluates near-surface forcing combinations for particle tracking in the East/Japan Sea using 33 GPS-tracked surface drifters deployed off the Korean coast in November 2021. The drifters revealed a clear cross-basin transport pathway toward the Japanese coastline and an early bifurcation near a hyperbolic-type Lagrangian structure. We used a particle tracking model to test combinations of geostrophic current, Ekman current, Stokes drift, and windage against the observed drifter trajectories using MAE and NCLS metrics. For undrogued DX drifters, the best performance was obtained by combining geostrophic current, Stokes drift, and windage. For drogued DO drifters, which sampled a deeper effective depth, the inclusion of all four forcing components provided the best agreement with observations. The optimized forcing combinations outperformed benchmark simulations driven by the GOFS analysis and GLORYS reanalysis in this specific event, primarily because they better reproduced the Lagrangian structure associated with the early trajectory bifurcation. Seasonal simulations showed that winter winds enhance eastward transport and beaching along Japan, whereas weaker summer winds allow mesoscale eddies to broaden particle dispersion. The present framework represents the fluid-dynamical component of near-surface particle transport and does not explicitly include plastic-specific fate processes such as fragmentation, biofouling-induced buoyancy changes, aggregation, settling, or resuspension.
