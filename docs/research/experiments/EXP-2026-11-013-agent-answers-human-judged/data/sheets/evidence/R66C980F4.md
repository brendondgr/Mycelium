## Question

## Answer

The evidence provided does not contain a specific project explicitly titled the "Marine heatwave early warning project." However, several studies and initiatives described in the evidence outline goals related to marine heatwave early warning, detection, and forecasting:

*   One study aims to provide a practical forecasting tool blueprint to optimize monitoring surveys, guide proactive triage, and support climate-adaptive Marine Spatial Planning under accelerating ocean warming #2.
*   Another study seeks to strengthen early-warning capabilities by highlighting the value of coupling regional ocean models with mesoscale eddy diagnostics to advance understanding of marine heatwave occurrences and drivers #6.

Setting (Mycelium's reading): Mediterranean coralligenous reefs, utilizing a historical record of mass mortality events from 1986 to 2020.
Abstract:
Accelerating marine heatwaves are outpacing static conservation strategies, threatening the functional integrity of temperate reefs globally. Implementing “climate-smart” Marine Spatial Planning requires moving beyond retrospective impact assessment toward prospective tools that can integrate local context to anticipate vulnerability. This study presents an explainable, policy-aware early warning system that leverages the most comprehensive historical record of mass mortality events (1986-2020) to forecast site-level risk in the Mediterranean coralligenous reefs. Using a rigorous modelling framework validated on independent prospective test data (2019-2020), the model achieves high forecasting skill (average precision = 0.75, where 1.0 is perfect and the baseline is 0.70) and maintains temporal robustness by simulating real-world forecasting across the historical record. Model interpretability indicates that macroclimatic thermal stress is modulated by local factors such as depth and spatial protection. For instance, each decade since marine protected area (MPA) designation corresponds to an estimated ~48% decrease in the odds of elevated mortality (95% CI: 17–72%) under high heat stress, although this modulating signal was statistically detectable primarily in the Western Mediterranean and does not imply absolute resistance against extreme thermal anomalies. By translating these complex, context-dependent predictions into equitable, subregion-balanced alerts through an open-access interactive decision-support platform, this study provides a practical forecasting tool blueprint to optimize monitoring surveys, guide proactive triage, and support climate-adaptive Marine Spatial Planning under accelerating ocean warming.

### Evidence #3 [paper] Mitigating Summertime Marine Heatwaves via Targeted Marine Cloud Brightening
Authors: Matthew T. Luongo, Edward Blanchard‐Wrigglesworth, Kyle Armour, Haruki Hirasawa, Jacob Cohen, Aaron Donohoe
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.31223/x5kv4t
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Marine heatwaves (MHWs) negatively impact marine ecosystems and coastal economies. Here, we evaluate whether a preemptive, short-term, localized surface emission of sea salt aerosols (marine cloud brightening, MCB) can mitigate the impacts of a predicted MHW without creating significant effects beyond the intervention zone. As a case study, we apply MCB to the 2019 Northeast Pacific MHW, "The Blob 2.0," using a global climate model seasonal-to-multiyear predictability ensemble. Initiating MCB in May 2019 mitigates important negative consequences of the event, substantially reducing the MHW magnitude and limiting associated declines in surface oxygen. The impacts of this intervention are mostly local and limited to summer, even when MCB is extended into winter, with nonlocal climate effects smaller than internal variability. By demonstrating local efficacy and minor remote impacts for mitigating the Blob 2.0, this work motivates further study of whether localized and temporary climate interventions can safely and effectively mitigate MHWs.

### Evidence #4 [paper] The evolution of the Dutch heat early warning system (2007 - 2025)
Authors: Carolina Pereira Marghidan, Rob Sluijter, Justine Blanford, Hisso Homan, Peter Siegmund, Werner Hagens, Maarten van Aalst
Venue: Climate Services
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cliser.2026.100718
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Heat early warning systems (HEWS) are critical tools for anticipating hazardous heat and enabling timely response. While some of the earliest systems were developed over two decades ago, little is known about how and why systems have evolved in practice. This study therefore examines the evolution of the Dutch heat warning system from 2007 to 2025 and evaluates the future performance of current warning criteria. We analysed 18 operational documents, including seven post-event evaluations, to identify operational challenges and drivers of change. Current warning criteria were also applied to KNMI’23 climate scenarios to assess future warning frequency, duration, intensity, and season length. Results show that the warning system evolved from a single trigger for the national Heat-Health Action Plan into a tiered warning system, while the HHAP remained linked to lower warning levels, creating misalignment between warnings and actions. Criteria remained primarily climatological due to limited epidemiological evidence, and criteria changes, such as the removal of nighttime criteria, were driven primarily by operational considerations. Future projections indicate a substantial increase in heat warnings, from ~2 per year currently to 3.9–7 by 2050 and up to 29 by 2100. The findings provide lessons for meteorological and public health agencies by showing how operational, institutional, climate, and scientific factors shape the design of heat early warning systems.

### Evidence #5 [paper] Rethinking Marine Heatwaves: Towards a Probabilistic Marine Heat Stress

Problem (Mycelium's reading): Standard marine heatwave (MHW) definitions rely on statistical temperature anomalies relative to a climatological baseline, which does not directly account for the thermal exposure experienced by marine organisms or its biological consequences.
Method (Mycelium's reading): The Probabilistic Marine Heat Stress Wave (PMHSW) framework, which integrates temperature exceedance above a biological threshold across time and depth to estimate cumulative thermal exposure and relates this exposure to the probability of a defined biological response.
Setting (Mycelium's reading): Synthetic examples used to demonstrate the estimation of response functions from biological observations and the derivation of binary event definitions.
Abstract:
Marine heatwaves (MHWs) are commonly defined as statistically extreme temperature events relative to a climatological baseline. While this approach provides a consistent description of ocean temperature extremes, it does not directly account for the thermal exposure experienced by marine organisms or its biological consequences. Here, we propose the Probabilistic Marine Heat Stress Wave (PMHSW) framework, which shifts the focus from temperature anomalies toward biologically relevant thermal exposure. The framework integrates temperature exceedance above a biological threshold across time and depth to estimate cumulative thermal exposure and relates this exposure to the probability of a defined biological response. The probabilistic formulation allows differences among organisms, habitats and thermal tolerances to be represented while retaining a continuous description of biological heat stress. Using synthetic examples, we demonstrate how the response function can be estimated from biological observations and how a binary event definition can be obtained when needed. The PMHSW framework is intended as a complementary approach to existing MHW definitions, particularly when the objective is to assess the potential biological consequences of marine thermal extremes under present and future climate conditions.

Abstract:
Marine heatwave (MHW) events are intensifying globally, doubling in frequency and increasing in duration and spatial distribution over recent decades. Semi-enclosed coastal basins are particularly vulnerable, and the Adriatic Sea (AS) exemplifies a fast-reacting system, making it a natural laboratory for studying climate-driven ocean variability and impacts on ecosystems, fisheries, and aquaculture. In this study, we assess the capability of a high-resolution Regional Ocean Modeling System (ROMS) to detect and simulate MHW events in the AS. We integrate this modeling framework with an eddy detection and tracking algorithm, alongside comprehensive analysis of long-term observational temperature data, to investigate the role of mesoscale eddies in modulating surface and subsurface MHW evolution. Beyond the Adriatic, this framework offers a transferable approach to other semi-enclosed and shelf seas, where reliable detection of MHW drivers is essential. By linking detection with predictive understanding, this work contributes to broader efforts to improve preparedness for marine extremes and safeguard ecosystems and dependent economies in a warming ocean.

Abstract:
Abstract As climate change drives increasingly frequent and severe climatic extremes, effective strategies to prevent extinction of threatened species are urgently needed. Interventions enabling species to survive acute stress may buy time to address other threats and identify long‐term refugia. For the Critically Endangered red handfish ( Thymichthys politus ), forecasts ahead of the 2023/2024 El Niño indicated a high risk of marine heatwaves (MHW). An expert workshop identified these forecasted extremes (>20 or +~3°C) as a severe threat, triggering an emergency rescue to temporarily remove individuals from the wild. This represents Australia's first pre‐emptive rescue of a protected marine species and highlights the importance of proactive, adaptive conservation to strengthen capacity in safeguarding threatened species during environmental extremes and long term climate change impacts.

Abstract:
Marine heatwaves (MHWs) have emerged as an increasingly severe disturbance to global marine ecosystems, with documented intensifications in both frequency and magnitude over the past century. Abstract Marine (MHWs) and atmospheric heatwaves (AHWs) are intensifying with climate change, but their co-occurrence as concurrent heatwaves (CHWs) poses an even greater threat to marine ecosystems. Method (Mycelium's reading): Analysis of Community Earth System Model version 2 simulations, ocean-assimilated experiments, and five-year hindcasts initialized annually from 1981 to 2020 to assess MHW frequency, cumulative intensity, and duration.
Setting (Mycelium's reading): 12 vulnerable coral reef regions across various ocean basins, using historical data from 1981 to 2020.
Abstract:
Marine heat waves (MHWs) increasingly threaten coral reefs, but their predictability beyond seasonal timescales remains poorly understood. Here we assess the seasonal-to-multi-year predictability of MHW frequency, cumulative intensity, and duration across 12 coral reef regions. Using Community Earth System Model version 2 simulations, ocean-assimilated experiments and five-year hindcasts initialized annually from 1981 to 2020, we show that frequency and cumulative intensity are predictable for several months to multiple years across many ocean basins, with skill varying strongly by region and season. Anthropogenic warming is the dominant source of multi-year skill across most hotspots, while internal variability limits predictability in the equatorial Pacific. El Niño-Southern Oscillation and Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation further modulate regional forecasts through coupled ocean-atmosphere processes. These findings reveal strong regional contrasts in the sources and limits of forecast skill, identifying where advance warning can support timely, targeted regional preparedness, coral reef conservation and adaptation to escalating thermal stress. The study shows that marine heatwave frequency and cumulative intensity across vulnerable coral reef regions are skillfully predictable from months to years ahead, with long-term anthropogenic warming accounting for most multi-year forecast skill, while internal variability, particularly El Niño, limits predictability in the equatorial Pacific.

### Evidence #17 [paper] Day-to-day atmospheric circulation modulated the impact of marine heatwaves on heatstroke risk in Hokkaido in August 2023
Authors: Takehiro Morioka, Tomonori Sato
Venue: SOLA
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.1007/s44393-026-00038-8
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Abstract Extreme high temperature and humidity threaten high-latitude regions that are less adapted to such conditions. While marine heatwaves (MHWs) can contribute to hot and humid conditions over land, the modulation of these impacts by the day-to-day atmospheric circulation remains poorly understood. This study focused on Hokkaido, the northernmost prefecture in Japan, to investigate how the daily atmospheric circulation regulated the distribution and intensity of MHW-related heatstroke risk during August 2023. Regional atmospheric model simulations indicated that the MHWs that developed south of Hokkaido increased the Wet Bulb Globe Temperature (WBGT) by approximately 2°C in southern coastal areas. Backward trajectory analysis showed that the intensity of MHW-induced heatstroke risk was governed by the daily atmospheric circulation. On days when MHWs substantially impacted the WBGT in the southeastern coastal region, air parcels passed over regions with high sea surface temperatures (SSTs). Conversely, when trajectories bypassed these MHW areas, the impact remained low. These results demonstrate that the daily atmospheric circulation can determine the magnitude of heatstroke risk over land. Our findings suggest that the relative positioning of airflow pathways and the SST distribution should be considered to improve heatstroke risk prediction in coastal regions.

### Evidence #18 [paper] From hazard-based to impact-based warnings: using social sensing to refine coastal flood thresholds in Maricá – RJ, Brazil
Authors: Camila Américo dos Santos, Fábio Ferreira Dias
Venue: Anthropocene Coasts
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.1007/s44218-026-00151-y
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Abstract Conventional coastal warning systems issue alerts based on regional oceanographic thresholds calibrated for navigation safety, generating a recurrent disconnection between physical hazard and documented urban impact. This study addresses that disconnection on the coast of Maricá (Rio de Janeiro, Brazil), a microtidal urban shoreline of reflective beaches exposed to South Atlantic swells. Hourly significant wave height ( Hs ) from the ERA5 reanalysis (2022–2025) was validated against the SiMCosta buoy CDRJ-1 ( R = 0.90; RMSE = 0.32 m under extreme conditions) and cross-referenced with 645 documentary records of storm surge episodes, compiled from institutional sources (Brazilian Navy, Maricá Civil Defense), local news portals, and citizen-generated Instagram content, consolidating 113 distinct events. Forecast skill metrics (POD, FAR, CSI) revealed contrasting source profiles: institutional alerts operate with FAR between 5.0% and 16.1% under a hazard-based threshold ( Hs ≥ 2.5 m), while citizen-generated records reached POD = 85.5% with FAR = 31.5%, capturing impacts that institutional logic excludes. In 2024, Maricá Civil Defense replaced the Brazilian Navy as the primary alert issuer. The analysis identified Hs ≥ 3.5 m as the critical threshold above which structural damage to urban infrastructure becomes recurrent, with damage frequency rising from 6.2% below 3.0 m to 50.0% above 3.5 m. Furthermore, 42.5% of documented events occurred below the operational threshold of 2.5 m, exposing the limitation of purely hazard-based warning logic. These results support the recalibration of local warning systems and the transition from hazard-based to impact-based logics on urbanised coasts.

### Evidence #19 [paper] Environmental controls on the detectability of hydrodynamic changes induced by offshore wind farm structures
Authors: Michela De Dominicis, Rory O’Hara Murray, Alejandro Gallego, Charlotte Anne June Williams, Beth E. Scott
Venue: Scientific Reports
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-71585-0
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Abstract A combination of model simulations and field observations collected in 2023 and 2024 near Seagreen, Scotland’s largest Offshore Wind Farm (OWF), is used to assess when, where and by how much (physical) changes induced by OWFs structures become detectable and environmentally relevant. Seagreen has the deepest (> 55 m) fixed bottom foundations of any OWF in the world (in 2026), and in offshore deep (> 50 m) waters there is the potential for OWFs structures to perturb natural ocean mixing and modify seasonal stratification. We found that OWF impacts on local hydrodynamics are highly sensitive to environmental conditions, with the strongest effects occurring under weakly stratified conditions, in frontal/transitional zones, when the water column is most responsive to small increases in mixing. Summer average sea surface temperature decreases are generally between -0.1 and -0.3 $$^{\circ }$$ C within the farm and in its wake, but with cooling exceeding -0.5 $$^{\circ }$$ C during the 2023 marine heatwave. Due to high seasonal and monthly variability, OWF impact assessments based on limited observational periods may underestimate or overestimate the range of hydrodynamic changes. This work highlights the value of combining numerical models with observations: model simulations identified the periods and locations of detectable OWF-induced changes, while observations supported the modelled response.
