## Question

## Answer

Based on the provided evidence, the three papers most directly relevant to a "Marine heatwave early warning project" are:

1.  **Multi-horizon forecasting of coastal high water temperature events for operational marine-heatwave early warning in South Korean aquaculture** (#3). This paper explicitly presents a "Hybrid GNN-BiLSTM" architecture designed for "operational marine-heatwave early warning" and demonstrates a "38 h mean advisory lead time" for coastal aquaculture, making it directly applicable to developing actionable early warning systems (#3).
2.  **FOCCUS WP9 D9.1 Application Demonstrator: ESC3 – 3.1.2 - Tracking sub-regional marine heatwaves in the Mediterranean Sea** (#16). This paper describes a web-based application that provides "near real-time detection and short-term forecasting of marine heatwaves" using Copernicus Marine satellite products and high-resolution regional model analysis, which serves as a practical blueprint for operational monitoring and forecasting tools (#16).
3.  **The evolution of the Dutch heat early warning system (2007 - 2025)** (#7). While focused on terrestrial heat, this study examines the evolution of a heat early warning system (HEWS) from a single trigger to a tiered system and evaluates how operational, institutional, and scientific factors shape system design, providing critical lessons for the design and implementation of any heat early warning system, including marine ones (#7).

Other papers, such as #1 (focusing on decision-maker interpretation of probabilistic predictions), #2 (proposing a new biological stress framework), #4 (describing a specific rescue intervention), #6 (focusing on reef mortality forecasting), #9 (analyzing exposure trends), #11 (using CMIP6 data for ML training), #12 (detecting MHWs in Indonesia), #13 (mitigating MHWs via cloud brightening), #14 (analyzing the 2023 North Atlantic event), #15 (detecting MHWs in the Adriatic), #17 (a duplicate of #16), #18 (reviewing ecosystem responses), #19 (forecasting emergency department demand), and #20 (analyzing concurrent heatwaves) provide supporting context but are less directly focused on the core operational early warning system design and forecasting methodology than the three selected above.

### Evidence #2 [paper] Rethinking Marine Heatwaves: Towards a Probabilistic Marine Heat Stress
Authors: Natalija Dunić, Krešimir Ruić, Ivica Vilibić
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.22541/essoar.15011394/v1
Still open/current: yes
Problem (Mycelium's reading): Standard marine heatwave (MHW) definitions rely on statistical temperature anomalies relative to a climatological baseline, which does not directly account for the thermal exposure experienced by marine organisms or its biological consequences.
Method (Mycelium's reading): The Probabilistic Marine Heat Stress Wave (PMHSW) framework, which integrates temperature exceedance above a biological threshold across time and depth to estimate cumulative thermal exposure and relates this exposure to the probability of a defined biological response.
Setting (Mycelium's reading): Synthetic examples used to demonstrate the estimation of response functions from biological observations and the derivation of binary event definitions.
Abstract:
Marine heatwaves (MHWs) are commonly defined as statistically extreme temperature events relative to a climatological baseline. While this approach provides a consistent description of ocean temperature extremes, it does not directly account for the thermal exposure experienced by marine organisms or its biological consequences. Here, we propose the Probabilistic Marine Heat Stress Wave (PMHSW) framework, which shifts the focus from temperature anomalies toward biologically relevant thermal exposure. The framework integrates temperature exceedance above a biological threshold across time and depth to estimate cumulative thermal exposure and relates this exposure to the probability of a defined biological response. The probabilistic formulation allows differences among organisms, habitats and thermal tolerances to be represented while retaining a continuous description of biological heat stress. Using synthetic examples, we demonstrate how the response function can be estimated from biological observations and how a binary event definition can be obtained when needed. The PMHSW framework is intended as a complementary approach to existing MHW definitions, particularly when the objective is to assess the potential biological consequences of marine thermal extremes under present and future climate conditions.

Abstract:
Abstract As climate change drives increasingly frequent and severe climatic extremes, effective strategies to prevent extinction of threatened species are urgently needed. Interventions enabling species to survive acute stress may buy time to address other threats and identify long‐term refugia. For the Critically Endangered red handfish ( Thymichthys politus ), forecasts ahead of the 2023/2024 El Niño indicated a high risk of marine heatwaves (MHW). An expert workshop identified these forecasted extremes (>20 or +~3°C) as a severe threat, triggering an emergency rescue to temporarily remove individuals from the wild. Following commonwealth, state and institutional approvals, 25 fish were captured in January 2024 and held in captivity for 3 months. During this period, in situ water temperatures exceeded records, including 80 days more than 2°C above the long‐term mean summer temperature (~17.5°C). Three fish died in captivity and four adults were retained in a captive breeding program, while the remaining 18 individuals were returned after the MHW subsided, with post‐release survival confirmed after 2 months. This represents Australia's first pre‐emptive rescue of a protected marine species and highlights the importance of proactive, adaptive conservation to strengthen capacity in safeguarding threatened species during environmental extremes and long term climate change impacts.

### Evidence #7 [paper] The evolution of the Dutch heat early warning system (2007 - 2025)
Authors: Carolina Pereira Marghidan, Rob Sluijter, Justine Blanford, Hisso Homan, Peter Siegmund, Werner Hagens, Maarten van Aalst
Venue: Climate Services
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cliser.2026.100718
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Heat early warning systems (HEWS) are critical tools for anticipating hazardous heat and enabling timely response. While some of the earliest systems were developed over two decades ago, little is known about how and why systems have evolved in practice. This study therefore examines the evolution of the Dutch heat warning system from 2007 to 2025 and evaluates the future performance of current warning criteria. We analysed 18 operational documents, including seven post-event evaluations, to identify operational challenges and drivers of change. Current warning criteria were also applied to KNMI’23 climate scenarios to assess future warning frequency, duration, intensity, and season length. Results show that the warning system evolved from a single trigger for the national Heat-Health Action Plan into a tiered warning system, while the HHAP remained linked to lower warning levels, creating misalignment between warnings and actions. Criteria remained primarily climatological due to limited epidemiological evidence, and criteria changes, such as the removal of nighttime criteria, were driven primarily by operational considerations. Future projections indicate a substantial increase in heat warnings, from ~2 per year currently to 3.9–7 by 2050 and up to 29 by 2100. The findings provide lessons for meteorological and public health agencies by showing how operational, institutional, climate, and scientific factors shape the design of heat early warning systems.

### Evidence #18 [paper] Understanding ecosystem responses to recurrent marine heatwaves

Venue: Biological Conservation
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biocon.2026.112016

Abstract:
Marine heatwaves (MHWs) have emerged as an increasingly severe disturbance to global marine ecosystems, with documented intensifications in both frequency and magnitude over the past century. Western Australia has experienced some of the most severe MHWs on record, causing widespread ecological impacts, including coral bleaching and mortality, local species extinctions, and socioeconomic impacts such as fishery closures, making the region a critical case study of climate-driven change in the marine domain. Most recently, a severe MHW during summer 2024/2025 has caused widespread disruption to marine life, reinforcing the region's vulnerability to repeated extreme events. While extensive research has documented the impacts of these events, most studies have examined individual heatwaves or specific taxonomic groups in isolation, limiting our understanding of cumulative and cascading effects across the ecosystem. Here, we combine a scoping review of published and emerging evidence with oceanographic analyses to assess the cumulative ecological impacts of MHWs across Western Australia. Using iconic MHWs as case studies, we reveal widespread ecological consequences, spanning habitat degradation, mass invertebrate mortality events, food web disruption, and spatial redistribution of fisheries' indicator species. Accordingly, we propose adaptive management strategies for future MHWs. These strategies include the relocation of vulnerable species, implementation of adaptive fisheries management frameworks, and development of rapid response monitoring programs targeting key benthic indicator groups. We emphasise that the successful implementation of such strategies hinges upon the accurate and timely prediction of future MHW events.

Abstract:
Objectives: Heatwaves drive surges in acute care demand, yet heat-health action plans (HHAPs) lack tools to scale emergency care responses. This study validated a forecasting tool for heatwave-related emergency department (ED) demand across four clusters comprising the emergency care network of Ticino Canton, Switzerland. Methods: This retrospective, ecological, time-series study modeled daily mean temperature and ED presentations using a distributed lag non-linear model framework with autoregressive and calendar adjustment components. Abstract:
Abstract Marine (MHWs) and atmospheric heatwaves (AHWs) are intensifying with climate change, but their co-occurrence as concurrent heatwaves (CHWs) poses an even greater threat to marine ecosystems. This study examines the spatiotemporal variability, physical drivers, and ecological impacts of CHWs across the marginal seas of the Arabian Peninsula from 1982 to 2021. CHWs are most frequent in the northern Red Sea, Arabian Gulf, and western Arabian Sea, and least common in the southern Red Sea. Their frequency and duration have increased significantly, by up to 7.5 days per decade in the northern Red Sea. Our findings show that CHWs are the dominant drivers of coral bleaching and productivity loss in the region, with 80–85% of severe bleaching events linked to CHWs. Case studies from 1998 and 2010 highlight the influence of high-pressure anomalies and weakened winds in shaping the widespread CHWs.
