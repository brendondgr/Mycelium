Venue: PLOS Sustainability and Transformation
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pstr.0000301
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Single-use plastics are embedded in daily life and contribute heavily to environmental impacts. Circular initiatives such as deposit return schemes aim to curb this problem yet remain under-researched despite their growing popularity. This study explored the barriers and enablers of engagement within a piloted deposit return scheme at University College London (UCL), in which participants paid a £1 deposit on reusable cups, receiving this refund upon return. Nine semi-structured interviews were conducted with UCL students and staff and analysed inductively to identify influences on behaviour, with themes subsequently mapped onto the Capability Opportunity Motivation-Behaviour (COM-B) Model. Thirteen themes were developed and organised under five COM-B constructs: Psychological capability (understanding the scheme; forgetting to return cups), Physical opportunity (cup design; accessibility of drop boxes; posters and instructions), Social opportunity (social interactions regarding the cups), Automatic motivation (habits; positive feelings) and Reflective motivation (perceived ease; perceived usefulness; perceived reliability; perceived hygiene; alternative goals for using the scheme). For example, for the scheme’s uptake, participants reported needing to understand the scheme, as well as perceiving it as hygienic and useful, and having constructive social interactions about it. Accessible drop locations were noted as particularly important for return behaviours, while forgetting cups or using them for other purposes (e.g., mementos) prevented reusable cups from functioning as shared, reusable assets. By applying a behaviour change framework, this study provides recommendations into how circular initiatives can be strengthened, for example, by providing clearer step-by-step instructions, enhancing communication by staff delivering the intervention and offering timely reminders to encourage cup returns. These insights can be used to inform similar schemes aiming to reduce single-use plastic consumption. The work also contributes to the growing body of evidence highlighting the need for strategies to manage packaging items at the sources, such as reducing and reusing cups, beyond recycling initiatives.

Abstract:
Community repair events bring materials science into contact with familiar products, but outreach teams must decide which objects deserve limited preparation time. This study evaluates topic selection under two representation rules: equal influence for each recorded repair attempt and equal influence for each repair group. The Open Repair Alliance collection contains 48,669 attempts. Selection used 17,805 attempts from 95 groups, followed by evaluation on 18,802 attempts from 126 groups. The four topics selected by attempt weighting were laptops, lamps, separate audio equipment and coffee makers. Group weighting replaced the latter two with mobile phones and small kitchen appliances. Its selection-period advantage in average group coverage contracted from 3.09 to 0.43 percentage points in the following year. Network-stratified group resampling gave a 95% interval of −1.29 to 2.28 points for that contrast, while attempt coverage decreased by 3.27 points. Local differences had opposing signs: 58 groups gained coverage, 63 lost coverage and five were unchanged. Resampling the selection groups recovered the complete topic sets in only 19.28% and 35.84% of draws. Increasing the kit to eight topics made the two rules select identical categories; ten topics were needed to exceed one-half of average group demand. These results establish the consequences of representation choices without attributing learning gains to category matching. A shared materials kit therefore requires an explicit intended beneficiary, host-specific preparation and an educational assessment independent of repair completion.

Abstract:
LCA presents important methodological gaps in the impact assessment phase, especially regarding the characterization factors (CF) applied for plastics. The purpose of this article is to identify methodological barriers and opportunities for improvement in the CFs proposed for plastics-related impacts. It discusses the need to advance toward regionalized characterization factors that better reflect the specific environmental, social, and technological conditions of different geographical contexts. ### Evidence #21 [paper] Changes in University Students’ Understanding of Plastic Challenges and Sustainability Competencies Following Internships

Venue: Education Sciences
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.3390/educsci16111865

Abstract:
This study explores changes in university students’ understanding of plastic-related environmental challenges and sustainability competencies following internships. Using a qualitative pre-post design, we analysed questionnaire responses from 16 students who completed six-week internships in Norwegian companies working with plastic challenges. Thematic analysis showed changes in knowledge related to environmental, societal, and economic impacts of marine plastics, and potential solutions and challenges. We identified changes in expressed knowledge related to disciplinary, critical and normative, creative and strategic, systems, and interpersonal and communication competencies. Students primarily demonstrated practice-oriented knowledge, such as familiarity with technologies, sustainability principles and stakeholder engagement, while reflective, ethical, and global dimensions remained less evident in students’ responses. We argue that higher education institutions should strengthen experiential learning and industry collaboration to link theory and practice and prepare students to contribute to sustainability work in practice.
