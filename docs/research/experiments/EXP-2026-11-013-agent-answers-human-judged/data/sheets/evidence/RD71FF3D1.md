## Question

What has entered our feed recently that matters for the Plastic pathways project, and what should we do about it this month?

## Answer

Based on the provided evidence, the following recent publications (all dated 2026) are relevant to the Plastic pathways project, along with recommended actions derived from their findings:

**1. Public Health and Exposure Management**
*   **New Evidence:** A secondary analysis of an international randomized controlled trial found that a brief 7-minute educational video significantly improved participants’ knowledge of plastic-related chemical risks and their short-term intention to reduce plastic use #4. However, sustained behavior change at a six-week follow-up was more strongly predicted by baseline intention than by the intervention itself #4.
*   **Action:** This month, integrate baseline motivation assessments into any educational campaigns, as education alone may not sustain behavior change without addressing pre-existing motivation and structural barriers #4.

**2. Agricultural and Food Supply Chain Impacts**
*   **New Evidence:** A review in *Frontiers in Plant Science* highlights that microplastics in croplands are causing phytotoxic effects, including inhibited germination and reduced photosynthetic efficiency, which may reduce staple crop production by tens to hundreds of millions of tons annually #12. The review argues that microplastic contamination should be reframed as a strategic stressor for global food system stability rather than just an ecotoxicological issue #12.
*   **Action:** Incorporate agricultural supply chain resilience and economic loss metrics into the project’s impact assessment, recognizing that plastic pollution has direct implications for food security and farm-level economics #12.

**3. Behavioral and Communication Barriers**
*   **New Evidence:** A qualitative study on a deposit return scheme for reusable cups at University College London identified that "forgetting to return cups" and a lack of understanding of the scheme were key barriers to engagement #1. The study recommends providing clearer step-by-step instructions and timely reminders to encourage returns #1.
*   **Action:** Review current communication materials for any circular economy initiatives within the project to ensure they include clear, step-by-step instructions and timely reminder mechanisms to combat user forgetfulness #1.

**4. Waste Management and Public Perception**
*   **New Evidence:** A survey of 3,135 Australian residents found that while the public is generally supportive of biosolids management, there is significant polarization regarding the use of biosolids in the food system #11. The study emphasizes the need for "soft infrastructure," such as policy and engagement processes, to build public confidence #11.
*   **Action:** Prioritize community engagement and transparent communication strategies to address public concerns and polarization regarding the reuse of waste products in food-related contexts #11.

**5. Microplastic Health Mechanisms**
*   **New Evidence:** Recent reviews indicate that micro- and nanoplastics can cross biological barriers, leading to oxidative stress, inflammation, and gut microbiome disruption, which may affect the gut-brain axis and lead to neuroinflammatory consequences #7 #9 #10.
*   **Action:** Ensure that health impact assessments in the project account for these specific mechanistic pathways, including gut dysbiosis and neuroinflammation, to provide a comprehensive picture of health risks #7 #10.

**6. Monitoring and Data Gaps**
*   **New Evidence:** Evidence for long-term health outcomes and causal relationships of microplastics in humans remains limited, and there is a lack of harmonized analytical methods and health-based exposure thresholds #7. Additionally, there is a severe geographical imbalance in the evidence base, with South Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, and Latin America underrepresented in field studies #12.
*   **Action:** Advocate for standardized monitoring protocols and prioritize data collection in underrepresented geographical regions to close these knowledge gaps #7 #12.

### Evidence #1 [paper] Understanding engagement with a deposit return scheme for reusable cups: A qualitative study on barriers and enablers of cup use and return through the COM-B Model
Authors: Isabel Warner, Ayşe Lisa Allison, Paulina M. Schenk
Venue: PLOS Sustainability and Transformation
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pstr.0000301
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Single-use plastics are embedded in daily life and contribute heavily to environmental impacts. Circular initiatives such as deposit return schemes aim to curb this problem yet remain under-researched despite their growing popularity. This study explored the barriers and enablers of engagement within a piloted deposit return scheme at University College London (UCL), in which participants paid a £1 deposit on reusable cups, receiving this refund upon return. Nine semi-structured interviews were conducted with UCL students and staff and analysed inductively to identify influences on behaviour, with themes subsequently mapped onto the Capability Opportunity Motivation-Behaviour (COM-B) Model. Thirteen themes were developed and organised under five COM-B constructs: Psychological capability (understanding the scheme; forgetting to return cups), Physical opportunity (cup design; accessibility of drop boxes; posters and instructions), Social opportunity (social interactions regarding the cups), Automatic motivation (habits; positive feelings) and Reflective motivation (perceived ease; perceived usefulness; perceived reliability; perceived hygiene; alternative goals for using the scheme). For example, for the scheme’s uptake, participants reported needing to understand the scheme, as well as perceiving it as hygienic and useful, and having constructive social interactions about it. Accessible drop locations were noted as particularly important for return behaviours, while forgetting cups or using them for other purposes (e.g., mementos) prevented reusable cups from functioning as shared, reusable assets. By applying a behaviour change framework, this study provides recommendations into how circular initiatives can be strengthened, for example, by providing clearer step-by-step instructions, enhancing communication by staff delivering the intervention and offering timely reminders to encourage cup returns. These insights can be used to inform similar schemes aiming to reduce single-use plastic consumption. The work also contributes to the growing body of evidence highlighting the need for strategies to manage packaging items at the sources, such as reducing and reusing cups, beyond recycling initiatives.

### Evidence #2 [paper] Household food-waste during events and social gatherings: a systematic review of consumer behavior, drivers, and reduction strategies
Authors: Zama P. Zibisi, Unathi Kolanisi, Cornelia Johanna Du Preez, Ruth Nachamada. Olusanya
Venue: Frontiers in Sustainability
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.3389/frsus.2026.1927306
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Introduction Household food waste during events and social gatherings is shaped by food management decisions as well as social and cultural expectations, yet evidence specific to these settings remains fragmented. This systematic review synthesised research on consumer knowledge, attitudes and practices, factors contributing to event-related household food waste, and reported reduction strategies. Methods Following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) framework, searches were conducted in Scopus, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, Google Scholar and SpringerLink for studies published from January 2020 to December 2025. Of 186 records identified, 44 duplicates were removed; 142 records underwent title and abstract screening, 53 full texts were assessed, and 32 studies were included in the final synthesis. Results The evidence base was geographically uneven: ten studies were from Europe, eight were international or multi-country, seven from Asia, four from Africa, two from North America and one from Australia. The thematic synthesis identified five connected themes: knowledge, attitudes, food management practices, behavioral and contextual drivers, and reduction strategies. Greater knowledge and positive attitudes were generally associated with more responsible food management, but awareness did not consistently translate into action. Poor meal planning, over-purchasing, excessive preparation, large portions, inadequate storage and ineffective leftover management contributed to surplus and avoidable disposal. During events, hospitality norms, cultural expectations, perceived generosity and uncertainty about guest numbers could outweigh intentions to prevent waste, encouraging hosts to prepare more food than was needed. Strategies reported across the literature included advance meal planning, shopping lists, portion control, improved storage, reuse of leftovers, food redistribution, consumer education and sustainable catering practices. Discussion Overall, the findings indicate that household food waste during events cannot be explained by individual knowledge or attitudes alone; it reflects the interaction of food management practices with social and contextual pressures. Event-specific evidence remains limited, particularly in African and other developing-country settings. Reduction efforts should therefore combine practical food-management skills with context sensitive approaches that support responsible hosting and address norms around food abundance.

### Evidence #3 [paper] Household-week measurement and cost accounting for community food provisioning: framework specification and partial pilot demonstration
Authors: Craig Steven Wright
Venue: Frontiers in Sustainable Food Systems
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.3389/fsufs.2026.1813246
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Community food projects commonly report production, expenditure, labour, water, or participation as separate totals. Such records do not show whether a defined service was offered and delivered to an eligible household in a particular service period. This Methods article specifies a household-week framework that joins six established streams—harvest, loss, distribution, labour participation, cost, and water—to a prospectively declared service obligation: recipient × service period × parameter set, with a weekly period as the default. It states the strongest objection to that design—that the join is only a fact-table grain, and that community-supported agriculture pick-up logs, food-bank visit records and humanitarian distribution monitoring already track recipients per cycle—and answers it: an event log cannot record a scheduled delivery that did not happen; demand-driven logs measure use rather than promise-keeping; and humanitarian distribution runs through relief supply chains and is monitored for donors, whereas a multi-year community project must join its own production, loss, labour, cost and water records to the same obligation. The join distinguishes production, offer, and delivery; identifies the first documented break; and calculates cost per eligible or successful obligation on one shared frame. Thresholds and cadence are declared parameters with threshold-response sensitivity, and provisioning reliability is separated from household food security. An 18-week pilot at one tropical site partially demonstrates the production pathway: recorded vegetable harvest totalled 1,421.63 kg, rising from 8.56 to 155.89 kg/week; the late five-week mean was 143.98 kg/week (SD 12.58); routine egg counts exist only for Weeks 43–44. Household delivery, complete loss, labour, full economic cost, and water records are absent, so continuity, cost per successful household-week, governance effects, waste reduction, and transferability are not pilot results. A complete schema, reference implementation and synthetic multi-period demonstration are supplied. This is the first, protocol-setting paper of a programme of five or more years, not a validated intervention.

### Evidence #4 [paper] Public knowledge of plastic-related chemical risks and intention to reduce exposure: a secondary analysis of an international randomized controlled trial
Authors: Danya Sandler, Rivka Green, Carly Goodman, Christine Till, Bruce Lanphear
Venue: BMC Public Health
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-29140-z
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Abstract Background Endocrine-disrupting chemicals in plastics are linked to earlier puberty, adverse birth outcomes, and reduced cognitive function, yet public awareness of these risks remains low. We examined whether a brief educational video could increase knowledge about plastic-related hazards and intention to reduce plastic use. Methods This secondary analysis draws from a parallel-group randomized controlled trial [1] in which 15 761 participants aged 18–45 were recruited from Canada, the United States, the United Kingdom, India, and Australia via CloudResearch's Prime Panels®. After completing baseline demographic and knowledge measures, participants were randomly assigned to watch a 7-minute educational video about toxic chemicals and neurodevelopment (n = 9135) or to view a blank screen (n = 6626). Immediate outcomes included three plastic-related knowledge items and one behavioural intention item assessing their likelihood of reducing plastic use in the next month. Following exclusions, 12 818 participants were included in analyses (intervention: n = 6427; control: n = 6391). Six weeks later, 3879 participants completed a follow-up assessing prevention attitudes and self-reported behaviour change. Results Participants who viewed the video were significantly more likely to report that plastics are harmful, disrupt hormone activity, and affect children’s cognitive development (rank-biserial r = .34–.38, all p < .001). They also reported greater intention to reduce plastic compared with controls (51% vs. 44.8%, (rank-biserial r = .06, p < .001). Intention was most strongly predicted by the belief that plastic chemicals disrupt hormone activity (OR = 1.99, p < .001). At six-week follow-up, higher baseline intention predicted greater self-reported reductions in plastic use (OR = 1.56, p < .001), with no significant difference by group. Effects varied across demographic subgroups, with stronger responses among participants in India, urban residents, South Asian participants, those with higher education or fewer children, and right-leaning participants. Conclusions A brief educational video improved plastics-related knowledge and short-term intentions to reduce exposure. However, behaviour change at follow-up was more strongly associated with baseline intention than with intervention status, highlighting the role of motivation and contextual factors in sustained behaviour change. Future interventions should consider baseline motivation and structural barriers alongside educational content.

### Evidence #9 [paper] Microplastic and Nanoplastic Exposures, Gut Microbiome Disruption, and Nutritional Health: A One Health Systematic Review of Human and Translational Evidence-Systematic Review

Venue: International Journal of Science and Research (IJSR)
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.21275/sr26917183702

Abstract:
Background: Food, drinking water, food-contact materials, and infant-feeding products are important interfaces for exposure to microplastics, nanoplastics, and associated contaminants. Their effects on the gut microbiome and host metabolism create a One Health problem linking nutritional, human, animal, and ecosystem health. Objective: To synthesize recent human and translational evidence on microplastic exposure, emphasizing gut microbiome disruption, nutritional pathways, One Health, and multi-omics. Methods: PubMed/MEDLINE and Europe PMC were searched for English-language reports published from January 1, 2021, through September 12, 2026; supplementary citation searching identified additional reports. ### Evidence #10 [paper] Microplastics and nano-plastics as emerging gut–brain axis disruptors: mechanistic insights, health implications, and future direction
Authors: Swagat Suman Patra, Sunita Panda, Jyotirmayee Turuk, Sanghamitra Pati
Venue: Frontiers in Public Health
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1885585
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Plastics are synthetic polymers widely used for their versatility, longer shelf life, and low cost. However, the non-biodegradable nature of conventional plastics has led to accumulation of microplastics (MPs; &amp;lt;5mm) and nanoplastics (NPs; &amp;lt;100 nm) in aquatic, terrestrial, and atmospheric environments. MNPs are composed of polyethene, polypropylene, polystyrene, polyvinyl chloride, and polyethene terephthalate. Human exposure to micro and nanoplastics occurs mainly through ingestion, inhalation, and rarely through dermal contact. The gastrointestinal tract is the potential accumulation site for such particles, which leads to microbial dysbiosis, disturbs intestinal barrier integrity and induces oxidative stress and inflammation. These disruptions lead to diseases related to the gastrointestinal tract, chronic systemic inflammation and affect the gut-brain axis with neuroinflammatory consequences. This review highlights gut dysbiosis and intestinal barrier disruption as a crucial gateway mediating the systemic effects of MNPs. On exposure to MNPs, gut microbial composition is altered by reducing beneficial short-chain fatty acid-producing bacteria such as Faecalibacterium, Roseburia , and Bifidobacterium , while stimulating the growth of opportunistic pathogens such as Desulfovibrio spp. Subsequent reductions in short-chain fatty acid generation induce lipopolysaccharide release and downregulation of tight junction proteins that compromise intestinal barrier integrity and expedite the translocation of MNPs, microbial products, and pro-inflammatory determinants into systemic circulation. MNPs then cross the blood-brain barrier by receptor-mediated routes, paracellular diffusion, or transcytosis, which is exacerbated by inflammation and oxidative stress. Excessive ROS further cause mitochondrial dysfunction, neuroinflammation, microglial activation, neurotransmitter imbalance, and pathological protein aggregation. This is a narrative review, focusing on integrating current evidence on the role of MNPs in disturbing the gut-brain axis through gut dysbiosis, intestinal barrier dysfunction, increased oxidative stress, inflammation, and altered neurochemical signaling. It further highlights the consequences of these changes in systemic health, identifies key knowledge gaps, and outlines future research priorities to better understand and mitigate the health risks.

### Evidence #12 [paper] Microplastic contamination in agricultural systems: impacts on plant productivity, economic losses, and supply chain resilience
Authors: Tong Xu
Venue: Frontiers in Plant Science
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2026.1956066
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Microplastics (MPs) have transitioned from a predominantly marine concern to a defining threat facing terrestrial agroecosystems, where croplands now function as one of the largest global reservoirs of plastic particles. This review synthesizes evidence to construct an integrated narrative that links the entry of MPs into farmland with declining plant productivity, quantifiable economic losses, and the erosion of agricultural supply chain resilience. The synthesis is based on a documented search of Web of Science, Scopus, ScienceDirect and Google Scholar covering January 2018 to March 2026, with explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria reported in Section 1.1. Sources, fate and soil-phase processes are treated in condensed form, because they are already covered comprehensively elsewhere; we retain only the evidence that propagates into crop, economic and supply-chain outcomes. Building on these soil-level disturbances, we evaluate the phytotoxic consequences for crops, spanning inhibited germination, impaired root architecture, reduced photosynthetic efficiency, disrupted nutrient acquisition, and the uptake and translocation of nano-sized particles into edible tissues. A central contribution of this review is the explicit coupling of these biophysical impacts to their economic and systemic dimensions, because recent global meta-analyses estimate that MP-induced photosynthetic suppression alone may reduce staple crop production by tens to hundreds of millions of tons annually, translating into substantial revenue losses, quality downgrading, remediation expenditure, and heightened exposure of food supply chains to shocks. Rather than reporting these modelled outputs at face value, we dissect the inferential chain that produces them, from effect size to biomass to harvestable yield to price, and we identify the assumptions at each step that determine whether the resulting figures are decision-grade. We complement the global estimates with farm-level cost–benefit evidence showing that the private returns to plasticulture remain positive while the contamination externality contributes under one percent of the accounted benefits of current management, which explains the persistence of practices that degrade the productive base. Throughout, illustrative case studies from China, Chile, Australia, and Europe are embedded within each thematic section rather than treated in isolation, demonstrating how local contamination patterns propagate into landscape- and market-scale consequences. We further appraise mitigation and remediation options, ranging from source reduction and material substitution to biochar amendment, and we assess emerging governance instruments such as the FAO Voluntary Code of Conduct and circular-economy regulation. Finally, we identify persistent knowledge gaps concerning the calibration of field-realistic dose–response relationships, the construction of decision-grade economic estimates, and the severe geographical imbalance of the evidence base, in which South Asia, sub-Saharan Africa and Latin America are collectively represented by a small fraction of published field studies, and we outline research priorities to safeguard soil health and food security. The review argues that microplastic contamination should be reframed not merely as an ecotoxicological problem but as a slow-onset stressor with strategic implications for the stability of global food systems.

Abstract:
