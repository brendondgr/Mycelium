## Question

Which three papers from the feed should the team on the Marine heatwave early warning project read first?

## Answer

Here are the three I'd put first on the team's reading list, in order:

**1. #4 — Evaluation of 1-14-Day Machine-Learning Forecasts of Marine Heatwaves in the Northern Bay of Bengal Against Persistence Benchmarks**
This is the closest match to what the project actually does: ML-based MHW forecasting, evaluated against a persistence benchmark over a 1–14 day lead time. It's a 2026 Zenodo release (code, data and results from a University of Dhaka B.S. thesis), so it's a concrete, reproducible example of the exact evaluation setup your team would want to compare against or build on.

**2. #3 — Marine heatwave forecast triggers rapid response strategy for critically endangered red handfish**
This is the "why" side of early warning: a real case where a forecast of an El Niño-driven MHW triggered a pre-emptive rescue of a critically endangered species in Australia (25 fish captured in January 2024, held through a record-breaking heatwave, 18 returned alive). It's a strong illustration of what a forecast is *for* — the decision and response chain that turns a prediction into action — and useful for framing the project's value beyond skill scores.

**3. #5 — Rescene-ESM: an Earth system model built around a frozen AI weather model**
The most methodologically interesting of the three: an Earth system model that yokes a frozen 20.8M-parameter AI weather model to physically formulated ocean, sea-ice, land and river components, reproducing El Niño variability and running ~1,000 years per day on one GPU. It's relevant to the project's longer-term horizon — how ML components can be embedded in physically constrained systems for scenario and ensemble exploration, which is where MHW early warning will eventually need to draw its climate context.

The rest of the slate is more tangential to this project: #6 and #9 are ocean/Arctic forecast system evaluations, #7 and #12 are long-term observational climatologies, and #10–#11 are SWOT sea-surface-height spectral work. Happy to pull the details on any of those if you want a second pass.

#3 [paper] Marine heatwave forecast triggers rapid response strategy for critically endangered red handfish
Authors: Jemina Stuart‐Smith, Tyson J. Bessell, Alistair James Hobday, Rick D. Stuart‐Smith, Ness Delpero, Victor Shelamoff, SD Ling, Tara Kelly
Venue: Conservation Science and Practice
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.1111/csp2.70439
Still open/current: yes
Problem (Mycelium's reading): Preventing extinction of the Critically Endangered red handfish (Thymichthys politus) during acute marine heatwaves (MHW) driven by climate change.
Method (Mycelium's reading): A proactive, pre-emptive rescue strategy triggered by climate forecasts and expert workshop assessment, involving the temporary capture of wild individuals, holding them in captivity for three months, and releasing them after the heatwave subsided.
Setting (Mycelium's reading): Wild population of red handfish in Australia during the 2023/2024 El Niño event, specifically January 2024, with in situ water temperatures exceeding records.
Abstract:
Abstract As climate change drives increasingly frequent and severe climatic extremes, effective strategies to prevent extinction of threatened species are urgently needed. Interventions enabling species to survive acute stress may buy time to address other threats and identify long‐term refugia. For the Critically Endangered red handfish ( Thymichthys politus ), forecasts ahead of the 2023/2024 El Niño indicated a high risk of marine heatwaves (MHW). An expert workshop identified these forecasted extremes (>20 or +~3°C) as a severe threat, triggering an emergency rescue to temporarily remove individuals from the wild. Following commonwealth, state and institutional approvals, 25 fish were captured in January 2024 and held in captivity for 3 months. During this period, in situ water temperatures exceeded records, including 80 days more than 2°C above the long‐term mean summer temperature (~17.5°C). Three fish died in captivity and four adults were retained in a captive breeding program, while the remaining 18 individuals were returned after the MHW subsided, with post‐release survival confirmed after 2 months. This represents Australia's first pre‐emptive rescue of a protected marine species and highlights the importance of proactive, adaptive conservation to strengthen capacity in safeguarding threatened species during environmental extremes and long term climate change impacts.

Abstract:
Earth system models underpin climate projections, but their cost limits how many scenarios, ensemble members and impact pathways can be explored. Machine-learning weather models forecast as skillfully as physics-based models at a fraction of the cost, yet coupled to an ocean they have produced weak El Nino variability or have learned it from climate-model output, and none carries the climate through rivers to the people exposed to it. Here we present Rescene-ESM, an Earth system model whose coupler, YAHO, yokes a frozen 20.8-million-parameter AI weather model to physically formulated ocean, sea-ice, land, river, carbon and energy components constrained by observations. Adding the couplings one at a time shows what each contributes: over a climatological ocean the AI atmosphere varies from year to year by a third of the observed amount over the oceans, and an interactive mixed layer, an equatorial wave ocean, the residual of the observed sea surface temperature (SST) equation as stochastic forcing and the land's moisture flux raise this step by step and bring El Nino and its teleconnections, leaving the mean climate almost unchanged. The full model simulates about 1,000 years per day on one graphics processing unit, holds the observed climate for a thousand years without drift, reproduces the observed 1979-2022 global warming, carries forced change with a physically based structure around the frozen network (transient climate response 1.60 K), routes runoff to 212 river gauges with a median seasonal-cycle correlation of 0.79 and, driven by emissions, separates the heatwave exposure of an SSP1 and an SSP3 world by a factor of 4.7 in 2081-2100, three-quarters of it through the warmer climate.
