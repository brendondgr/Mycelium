About the project How did early medieval societies use the letter and who were among its users? The Lettercraft-project approaches the early medieval letter as a performative medium, whose communicative potential was defined principally by non-textual features: its materiality, its ability to be publicly read out and talked over, and its potential quickly to reach wide and diverse audiences. The project submits that the letter was a far more inclusive medium than is commonly assumed on the basis of its textual qualities. Literacy or social status were not required to participate in what the project calls lettercraft, i.e. the various performative actions (e.g. listening, speaking, negotiating, gossiping) that along with writing and reading made up epistolary communication. The project aims to show, moreover, that due to its public nature and social range, the letter was an essential tool of community-building and conflict-resolution in medieval society. As long as it was properly performed, a single letter could persuade an entire community. The project studies the performative potential of the letter in the context of the Merovingian Kingdoms, 476-751 CE, whose communicative networks covered much of Europe. Innovatively, it relies not on the surviving letter-collections, which showcase the literary exploits of elite ecclesiastical men, but rather on descriptions of lettercraft in Merovingian histories and saint-lives. The project proceeds along two complementary pathways. First, it has established an index of Merovingian epistolary terminology that is sensitive to all (textual and non-textual) aspects of lettercraft. This index can be consulted in the glossary provided on the Lettercraft website. We plan to include it in this dataset in the future. Second, the project is developing a new open access dataset of Merovingian epistolary communication, which records descriptions of letter-use documented in Merovingian narrative sources. Significantly, it is a dataset not of letters and their textual contents, but of the non-textual ‘metadata’ surrounding the epistolary process, including references to participants, delivery, performance, gift-giving, materiality, and audience. This enables users to analyses the social contexts in which early medieval epistolary communication took place, the diverse individuals and groups who participated in it, and the actions they performed. About the dataset Corpus – This dataset contains annotations on the use of letters in early medieval narrative texts. The project’s principal source are the eight volumes of Merovingian narrative texts edited by Bruno Krusch and Wilhelm Levison for the Monumenta Germaniae Historica under the header Scriptores rerum Merovingicarum (1885-1951), complemented with a smaller corpus of Merovingian texts not edited in this collection. Associate Professor, Department of Government The rise of far-right antidemocratic actors in many consolidated democracies today poses a unique challenge. Political parties and leaders who enjoy popular support risk tearing apart the democratic fabric through tactics that appear lawful and are often invisible. What options do democracies have to defend themselves against such actors? Democratic self-defence, i.e. actions aimed at preventing, containing, resisting, or repairing democratic backsliding caused by domestic antidemocratic forces, can take many forms: from restrictions on candidacy rights to hate speech laws, and from street protests to welfare reforms. Political parties may exclude antidemocratic parties from coalitions, or they may choose to include them while seeking to moderate their positions. Government shutdowns impose enormous costs on the economy and on federal workers, and yet they keep happening, and they keep getting longer. Why? The existing literature treats shutdowns as either a byproduct of legislative gridlock or a symptom of partisan polarization, but neither account explains why rational political actors repeatedly choose an outcome that nobody wants. This paper argues that shutdowns are not bargaining failures but strategic equilibrium outcomes, sustained by the political cost of being seen to back down. I model government shutdown as a repeated Chicken game between Democrats and Republicans with three parameters: the policy benefit of winning (B), the cost of the shutdown itself (S), and the audience cost a leader pays for conceding after a public commitment (L), the political penalty for being the one who concedes. When L exceeds S, holding firm dominates conceding for both sides, and the mutual-shutdown outcome becomes the equilibrium. Because shutdown costs accumulate over time, duration depends not only on how high L is but on how quickly S(t) rises to meet it. Across repeated rounds, each concession compounds the reputational cost of conceding in the next round, producing a ratchet effect. I test the model against six cases, the 1978, 1995–96, 2013, 2018–19, and 2025 shutdowns, plus the 2011 near-shutdown, using process tracing and structured comparison.
