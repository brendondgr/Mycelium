## Answer

The provided evidence does not explicitly state what is "blocking" the Marine heatwave early warning project, as the documents describe various research efforts, frameworks, and case studies rather than a single specific project with identified administrative or technical blockers. However, the evidence highlights several challenges and limitations in current methodologies that could be interpreted as obstacles to effective early warning systems.

One significant challenge is that standard marine heatwave definitions rely on statistical temperature anomalies relative to a climatological baseline, which do not directly account for the thermal exposure experienced by marine organisms or their biological consequences #4. Additionally, satellite-derived sea surface temperatures cannot always be used to estimate the spatial structure, duration, intensity, and drivers for temperatures below the ocean surface, indicating a gap in subsurface monitoring #8.

Several papers in the feed offer methods that could help address these issues. The Probabilistic Marine Heat Stress Wave (PMHSW) framework integrates temperature exceedance above a biological threshold across time and depth to estimate cumulative thermal exposure, which addresses the limitation of standard statistical definitions #4. To address the lack of subsurface data, a study using near-continuous autonomous underwater glider observations in the North Sea demonstrates that sustained glider observations can provide key data alongside satellite and reanalysis products to improve the analysis of subsurface temperature anomalies #8. Furthermore, a study on the Adriatic Sea highlights the value of coupling regional ocean models with mesoscale eddy diagnostics to advance understanding of marine heatwave occurrences and drivers, which can strengthen early-warning capabilities #7. Finally, a multi-horizon forecasting study for South Korean aquaculture presents a Hybrid GNN-BiLSTM model that achieves high forecasting skill and provides operationally actionable early warning, addressing the need for site-specific temporal granularity #19.

Abstract:
Marine heatwaves (MHWs) are commonly defined as statistically extreme temperature events relative to a climatological baseline. While this approach provides a consistent description of ocean temperature extremes, it does not directly account for the thermal exposure experienced by marine organisms or its biological consequences. Here, we propose the Probabilistic Marine Heat Stress Wave (PMHSW) framework, which shifts the focus from temperature anomalies toward biologically relevant thermal exposure. The framework integrates temperature exceedance above a biological threshold across time and depth to estimate cumulative thermal exposure and relates this exposure to the probability of a defined biological response. The probabilistic formulation allows differences among organisms, habitats and thermal tolerances to be represented while retaining a continuous description of biological heat stress. Using synthetic examples, we demonstrate how the response function can be estimated from biological observations and how a binary event definition can be obtained when needed. The PMHSW framework is intended as a complementary approach to existing MHW definitions, particularly when the objective is to assess the potential biological consequences of marine thermal extremes under present and future climate conditions.

Introduction High water temperature (HWT) marine-heatwave events along the southern coast of South Korea cause repeated mass mortalities in cage aquaculture of olive flounder (Paralichthys olivaceus), rock bream (Oplegnathus fasciatus), red sea bream (Pagrus major) and Korean rockfish (Sebastes schlegelii). Existing operational forecasts rely on coarse numerical ocean models that lack the temporal granularity and site specificity required by farm managers, while the recent wave of deep-learning sea surface temperature (SST) models has been tuned almost exclusively on smoother open-ocean grids. Methods We present the Hybrid GNN-BiLSTM, a 3.1 M-parameter graph–temporal architecture combining a weighted spatial GNN with a BiLSTM temporal core, reversible instance normalization (RevIN) and a persistence skip connection, and benchmark it against ten alternatives – three reference baselines (Persistence, Seasonal-naïve, Climatology), the ARIMAX statistical model, four lightweight or linear deep models (DLinear, PatchTST, N-HiTS, CNN-LSTM) and two attention-based models (Informer, Temporal Fusion Transformer) – on a five-year hourly insitu panel from 30 monitoring stations along the Tongyeong–Geoje–Yeosu coast (2020–2025) under a unified rolling-origin walk-forward protocol (720 h lookback, 168 h horizon, one forecast per station every 24 h across a 12-month validation window). Marine-heatwave detection is scored at tiered 26/28/30 °C operational thresholds. Results The Hybrid GNN-BiLSTM achieves the lowest 1 h RMSE (0.057 °C), 0.85 °C at h = 72 and 1.24 °C at h = 168, with the highest long-horizon residual skill of any deep model on this panel (SS_res = 0.71 at h = 72). CNNLSTM attains the lowest mean horizon root-mean-square error (RMSE = 0.636 °C) thanks to a nearly flat error curve, and PatchTST is competitive at short-to-medium horizons (0.694 °C mean). The Hybrid GNN-BiLSTM reaches F1 = 0.82 at 26 °C and F1 = 0.65 at 28 °C, and a probabilistic Gaussian negative-log-likelihood head supplies calibrated 90 % prediction intervals without post-hoc recalibration. Discussion A 38 h mean advisory lead time was demonstrated in the 2024 NIFS regional HWT alert period, indicating that a site-specific graph–temporal model can deliver operationally actionable early warning for coastal aquaculture.

### Evidence #20 [paper] Marine Heatwave Detection and Dynamics in the Indonesian Seas under ENSO and Indian Ocean Dipole Forcing

Problem (Mycelium's reading): Detection and analysis of Marine Heatwaves (MHWs) in the Indonesian seas, specifically examining their relationship with El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) and Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) forcing.
Method (Mycelium's reading): A reproducible Python workflow that acquires and preprocesses Optimum Interpolation Sea Surface Temperature (OISST) data, detects MHWs, acquires ENSO and IOD climate indices, and performs statistical summaries, sensitivity assessments on climatological baselines, and spatial composite analyses.
Setting (Mycelium's reading): Indonesian seas, using OISST sea surface temperature observations and climate indices for ENSO and IOD.
Abstract:
This repository contains a collection of Python scripts for detecting, quantifying, and analyzing Marine Heatwaves (MHWs) in the Indonesian seas, with particular emphasis on their relationship with large-scale climate variability associated with the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) and the Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD). The scripts provide a reproducible workflow for examining the spatial and temporal characteristics of marine heatwaves, including their frequency, duration, intensity, cumulative intensity, and spatial distribution. The workflow integrates sea surface temperature (SST) observations from the Optimum Interpolation Sea Surface Temperature (OISST) dataset with climate indices representing ENSO and IOD variability. The repository includes the following components: 1. OISST data acquisition and preprocessing. 2. Marine heatwave detection and event characterization. 3. Acquisition and processing of ENSO and IOD climate indices. 4. Statistical summaries, figures, and tables of MHW characteristics. 5. Comparison of alternative climatological baselines and sensitivity assessments. 6. Analysis of MHW temporal dynamics and long-term variability. 7. Investigation of regional climate drivers and sensitivity to ENSO and IOD conditions. 8. Spatial composite analysis to identify regional patterns and climate-related MHW responses. The scripts are designed to support reproducible climate-ocean research and provide a framework for understanding how large-scale climate modes influence marine heatwave variability across the Indonesian seas. This repository is intended for researchers, oceanographers, climate scientists, and students working on marine climate variability, ocean warming, extreme ocean events, and tropical ocean-atmosphere interactions. The code can be adapted to other marine regions and extended with additional oceanographic datasets and climate indices.
