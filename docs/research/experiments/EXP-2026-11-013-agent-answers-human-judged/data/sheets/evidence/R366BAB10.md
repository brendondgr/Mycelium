## Question

Which open grant in our feed fits the Plastic pathways project best?

## Answer

Based on the provided evidence, the grant that fits the "Plastic pathways" project best is the **Developing novel recycling technologies for complex plastic materials applying biotech solutions** grant #17.

This grant explicitly supports proposals that advance novel biotech solutions for the recycling of complex plastic materials and products #17. It requires the development of solutions that consider the latest material innovations, such as the application of microbes and enzymes for enhanced degradation of plastics #17. The scope of this grant aligns with the goal of managing plastic pathways by focusing on the recycling of multi-material, multi-layer, and composite plastics in sectors like packaging and construction #17.

Another potentially relevant option is the **Innovative circular solutions for end-of-life footwear through collection, sorting and recycling** grant #14, which aims to improve the circularity of the footwear value chain and reduce the environmental footprint of end-of-life post-consumer footwear #14. This grant also involves developing methods to minimize the release of hazardous chemicals and microplastics during the recycling process #14. However, the biotech recycling grant #17 is broader in its application to "complex plastic materials" generally, whereas the footwear grant is specific to that product category.

### Evidence #2 [paper] Comparative analysis of barriers and opportunities for implementation of blue-green infrastructure for enhancing urban flood resilience in Uganda, Rwanda, and Sweden

Venue: Cities
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cities.2026.107713
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Recent studies have identified a combination of socio-economic, institutional, governance and financing barriers that constrain the adoption of Blue-Green Infrastructure (BGI) for enhancing urban flood resilience and provision of multiple benefits in cities. However, most barrier analysis studies have used literature reviews, semi-structured interviews, or questionnaire surveys in case studies in the Global North; in contrast, there is limited research and uptake of BGI in flood prone cities in Sub-Saharan Africa. This study addresses these gaps by conducting a comparative analysis of barriers and opportunities for BGI implementation in two flood prone Sub Saharan African cities (Kampala, Uganda; and Kigali, Rwanda), and one Global North city (Lund, Sweden), with the aim of supporting strategies for BGI implementation and identifying transferable best practices and learning opportunities. The study identified several opportunities to increase BGI uptake, including land availability, using public-private partnerships for BGI implementation, and knowledge of the benefits of BGI. In addition, research on multifunctional effects can facilitate funding and implementation of new funding mechanisms, such as tax breaks or reduced utility fees for property owners. The identified barriers, opportunities, and proposed methodological framework can be used to develop strategies for the uptake of BGI to contribute to resilient and sustainable urban water management.

### Evidence #3 [paper] Understanding engagement with a deposit return scheme for reusable cups: A qualitative study on barriers and enablers of cup use and return through the COM-B Model
Authors: Isabel Warner, Ayşe Lisa Allison, Paulina M. Schenk
Venue: PLOS Sustainability and Transformation
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pstr.0000301
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Single-use plastics are embedded in daily life and contribute heavily to environmental impacts. Circular initiatives such as deposit return schemes aim to curb this problem yet remain under-researched despite their growing popularity. This study explored the barriers and enablers of engagement within a piloted deposit return scheme at University College London (UCL), in which participants paid a £1 deposit on reusable cups, receiving this refund upon return. Nine semi-structured interviews were conducted with UCL students and staff and analysed inductively to identify influences on behaviour, with themes subsequently mapped onto the Capability Opportunity Motivation-Behaviour (COM-B) Model. Thirteen themes were developed and organised under five COM-B constructs: Psychological capability (understanding the scheme; forgetting to return cups), Physical opportunity (cup design; accessibility of drop boxes; posters and instructions), Social opportunity (social interactions regarding the cups), Automatic motivation (habits; positive feelings) and Reflective motivation (perceived ease; perceived usefulness; perceived reliability; perceived hygiene; alternative goals for using the scheme). For example, for the scheme’s uptake, participants reported needing to understand the scheme, as well as perceiving it as hygienic and useful, and having constructive social interactions about it. Accessible drop locations were noted as particularly important for return behaviours, while forgetting cups or using them for other purposes (e.g., mementos) prevented reusable cups from functioning as shared, reusable assets. By applying a behaviour change framework, this study provides recommendations into how circular initiatives can be strengthened, for example, by providing clearer step-by-step instructions, enhancing communication by staff delivering the intervention and offering timely reminders to encourage cup returns. These insights can be used to inform similar schemes aiming to reduce single-use plastic consumption. The work also contributes to the growing body of evidence highlighting the need for strategies to manage packaging items at the sources, such as reducing and reusing cups, beyond recycling initiatives.

### Evidence #5 [paper] Household-week measurement and cost accounting for community food provisioning: framework specification and partial pilot demonstration
Authors: Craig Steven Wright
Venue: Frontiers in Sustainable Food Systems
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.3389/fsufs.2026.1813246
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Community food projects commonly report production, expenditure, labour, water, or participation as separate totals. Such records do not show whether a defined service was offered and delivered to an eligible household in a particular service period. This Methods article specifies a household-week framework that joins six established streams—harvest, loss, distribution, labour participation, cost, and water—to a prospectively declared service obligation: recipient × service period × parameter set, with a weekly period as the default. It states the strongest objection to that design—that the join is only a fact-table grain, and that community-supported agriculture pick-up logs, food-bank visit records and humanitarian distribution monitoring already track recipients per cycle—and answers it: an event log cannot record a scheduled delivery that did not happen; demand-driven logs measure use rather than promise-keeping; and humanitarian distribution runs through relief supply chains and is monitored for donors, whereas a multi-year community project must join its own production, loss, labour, cost and water records to the same obligation. The join distinguishes production, offer, and delivery; identifies the first documented break; and calculates cost per eligible or successful obligation on one shared frame. Thresholds and cadence are declared parameters with threshold-response sensitivity, and provisioning reliability is separated from household food security. An 18-week pilot at one tropical site partially demonstrates the production pathway: recorded vegetable harvest totalled 1,421.63 kg, rising from 8.56 to 155.89 kg/week; the late five-week mean was 143.98 kg/week (SD 12.58); routine egg counts exist only for Weeks 43–44. Household delivery, complete loss, labour, full economic cost, and water records are absent, so continuity, cost per successful household-week, governance effects, waste reduction, and transferability are not pilot results. A complete schema, reference implementation and synthetic multi-period demonstration are supplied. This is the first, protocol-setting paper of a programme of five or more years, not a validated intervention.

Abstract:
Introduction Local public food procurement (LPFP) by hospital foodservice offers governments an opportunity to support local food systems and capitalise on multiple co-benefits. Facilitators included intuitive platform design, routines outside consultation time, nurse-physician collaboration, responsive support and evidence-informed recommendations. Conclusions: Uneven adoption of the Matter programme's digital pathway was not primarily explained by rejection of perinatal mental health screening, but by whether the pathway could be embedded in routine workflows and team-based care. Sustainable implementation requires role clarity, protected time, reliable technical infrastructure, local support and equity-oriented adaptations. ### Evidence #19 [paper] Funding mechanisms as incentive systems: a role for science philanthropy
Authors: Lillian Parr, Nadia Sarfraz, Sylvina M. Raver, Nadia M. Penrod
Venue: Frontiers in Systems Biology
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.3389/fsysb.2026.1939099
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Biomedical research is shaped not only by scientific questions, but also by the incentive structures that determine what scientists study, how they approach the work, and how success is defined. Current incentives often prioritize individual achievement as a pathway to career advancement, while undervaluing replication, data sharing, mentorship, collaboration, and shared infrastructure. This can make it challenging for scientists to prioritize these contributions, even when they recognize that they are essential for a strong scientific ecosystem. This article examines how funders and funding mechanisms create implicit incentives and argues that funding design is a powerful lever for shaping both scientific behavior and the broader research ecosystem. Philanthropy is uniquely positioned to experiment with funding design by supporting high-risk research, emerging areas, and investments in people, fields, norms, and shared infrastructure that other funders may be unable or unwilling to pursue, while also dedicating resources to learn from how these investments shape scientific behavior. Here, we highlight questions funders can ask about the incentives embedded in funding mechanisms, including what behaviors they encourage, who participates and benefits, and what persists beyond the award. For scientists, understanding funding goals, participating in evaluations, and making contributions beyond traditional outputs visible can strengthen the feedback between funding design and scientific practice. By treating funding mechanisms as design choices that can be tested and refined, philanthropy can generate evidence that informs future funding approaches and strengthens the scientific ecosystem.
