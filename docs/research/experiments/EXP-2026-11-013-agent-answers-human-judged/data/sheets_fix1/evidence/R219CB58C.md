Authors: Richard Harry, Shanshan Mei, DEAN BALL
Venue: Advanced Materials Technologies
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.1002/admt.71315
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
ABSTRACT Artificial intelligence (AI) is reshaping materials discovery into a more integrated pipeline by combining data generation, surrogate prediction, and, in selected settings, closed‐loop experimentation at an unprecedented rate. However, the institutional mechanisms needed to govern reproducibility, interoperability, and oversight across these workflows remain underdeveloped. This perspective examines how federally funded research and development centers (FFRDCs) can close that gap across three domains: data and infrastructure, algorithmic intelligence, and autonomous experimentation. Meanwhile, foreign competitors pursue centralized national materials programs of their own.

### Evidence #5 [paper] Funding mechanisms as incentive systems: a role for science philanthropy
Authors: Lillian Parr, Nadia Sarfraz, Sylvina M. Raver, Nadia M. Penrod
Venue: Frontiers in Systems Biology
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.3389/fsysb.2026.1939099
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Biomedical research is shaped not only by scientific questions, but also by the incentive structures that determine what scientists study, how they approach the work, and how success is defined. Current incentives often prioritize individual achievement as a pathway to career advancement, while undervaluing replication, data sharing, mentorship, collaboration, and shared infrastructure. This can make it challenging for scientists to prioritize these contributions, even when they recognize that they are essential for a strong scientific ecosystem. This article examines how funders and funding mechanisms create implicit incentives and argues that funding design is a powerful lever for shaping both scientific behavior and the broader research ecosystem. Philanthropy is uniquely positioned to experiment with funding design by supporting high-risk research, emerging areas, and investments in people, fields, norms, and shared infrastructure that other funders may be unable or unwilling to pursue, while also dedicating resources to learn from how these investments shape scientific behavior. Here, we highlight questions funders can ask about the incentives embedded in funding mechanisms, including what behaviors they encourage, who participates and benefits, and what persists beyond the award. For scientists, understanding funding goals, participating in evaluations, and making contributions beyond traditional outputs visible can strengthen the feedback between funding design and scientific practice. By treating funding mechanisms as design choices that can be tested and refined, philanthropy can generate evidence that informs future funding approaches and strengthens the scientific ecosystem.

Programs such as I-Corps and C3i improve commercialization literacy but primarily function as educational interventions. The NIH Blueprint MedTech (BPMT) Seedling Program addresses this gap by converting reviewer-identified commercialization weaknesses into structured, time-bounded work packages aligned with the Guidance and Impact Tracking System (GAITS), a commercialization maturity framework, and supported by experienced mentors. Methods The six-month, mentor-centered Seedling Program operates within two NIH-funded neurotechnology incubator hubs: the Center for Innovative NeuroTech Advancement (CINTA) and NeuroTech Harbor ( N TH). Following multidisciplinary review, weaknesses are mapped to GAITS maturity indicators across Technology, Regulatory, Market/Business, and Clinical domains and converted into evidence-generating Work Packages. This descriptive program evaluation analyzed gap distributions across three cohorts ( N = 58) and Deep Dive progression outcomes for two completed cohorts ( N = 39). Results GAITS-based review identified frequent commercialization gaps at program entry. Prospective studies should incorporate standardized pre/post maturity measures, external downstream outcomes, and appropriate comparison groups to evaluate program effectiveness.

### Evidence #14 [paper] Selecting Materials Outreach Topics from Community Repair Demand: Organizational Weighting, Temporal Transfer and Local Fit
Authors: Chahn Yong Jung
Venue: Journal of Materials Education (Electronic)
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.71448/jme2026346
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Community repair events bring materials science into contact with familiar products, but outreach teams must decide which objects deserve limited preparation time. This study evaluates topic selection under two representation rules: equal influence for each recorded repair attempt and equal influence for each repair group. The Open Repair Alliance collection contains 48,669 attempts. Selection used 17,805 attempts from 95 groups, followed by evaluation on 18,802 attempts from 126 groups. The four topics selected by attempt weighting were laptops, lamps, separate audio equipment and coffee makers. Group weighting replaced the latter two with mobile phones and small kitchen appliances. Its selection-period advantage in average group coverage contracted from 3.09 to 0.43 percentage points in the following year. Network-stratified group resampling gave a 95% interval of −1.29 to 2.28 points for that contrast, while attempt coverage decreased by 3.27 points. Local differences had opposing signs: 58 groups gained coverage, 63 lost coverage and five were unchanged. Resampling the selection groups recovered the complete topic sets in only 19.28% and 35.84% of draws. Increasing the kit to eight topics made the two rules select identical categories; ten topics were needed to exceed one-half of average group demand. These results establish the consequences of representation choices without attributing learning gains to category matching. A shared materials kit therefore requires an explicit intended beneficiary, host-specific preparation and an educational assessment independent of repair completion.

### Evidence #20 [paper] Researcher profiles, proposal submission, and project approval: an exploratory case study at a Brazilian Federal Institute
Authors: Woska Pires da Costa, Cristhian Chagas Ribeiro, Marcos de Moraes Sousa, Cleiton Mateus Sousa, Priscilla Rayanne e Silva, Kodhandaraman Chinnathambi, Manuel Monfort-Pañego, Mark A. Noll
Venue: Frontiers in Research Metrics and Analytics
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.3389/frma.2026.1888607
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Introduction Decisions to enter funding competitions and subsequent funding outcomes are connected components of a broader process. However, evidence on how researcher-profile characteristics relate to these stages remains limited in public research institutions outside the research-intensive university sector, particularly in emerging-economy research systems. Objective This exploratory case study analyzed the associations between researcher-profile characteristics and two respondent-level outcomes among researchers at a Brazilian Federal Institute: proposal submission and, among submitters, self-reported approval of at least one project. Methods An online cross-sectional survey was completed by 83 of 327 eligible researchers (response rate 25.4%) in 2023. Data from this self-selected, single-institution sample were analyzed using bivariate contingency-table tests, exact tests for sparse tables, and Cramér's V as a descriptive effect-size measure. Findings were treated as hypothesis-generating. Results Proposal submission was associated with education level ( p = 0.020; V = 0.285) and teaching in a master's or doctoral degree program ( p < 0.001; V = 0.409). Among respondents who submitted proposals, reporting at least one approved project was associated with age group ( p = 0.011; V = 0.403) and CNPq productivity fellowship ( PQ ) status ( p = 0.047; V = 0.284). The approval outcome was measured at the respondent level and did not represent a proposal-level success rate. Conclusion By distinguishing submission from approval, this study contributes to research evaluation by indicating that proposal submission and reporting at least one approved project among submitters were associated with different researcher-profile characteristics. These exploratory findings do not establish barriers or causal mechanisms. Multicenter, proposal-level, and longitudinal studies using explicit, context-sensitive definitions of career stage should test whether these patterns vary across institutions, fields, and career trajectories.
