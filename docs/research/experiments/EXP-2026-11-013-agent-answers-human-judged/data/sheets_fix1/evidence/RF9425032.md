## Question

What is the main idea of this paper, and who are its authors?

## Answer

The main idea of Rescene-ESM is the presentation of an Earth system model that uses a coupler called YAHO to link a frozen 20.8-million-parameter AI weather model to physically formulated components (energy, carbon, river, land, sea-ice, and ocean) constrained by observations #1. The model is designed to address the high computational costs of traditional Earth system models and the weak El Niño variability produced by existing machine-learning weather models coupled to oceans #1. The authors of the paper are Minjong Cheon and Kaoru Kakinuma #1.

Setting (Mycelium's reading): Simulations of approximately 1,000 years on one graphics processing unit; evaluation against observed climate data from 1979-2022; routing of runoff to 212 river gauges; and future projections for SSP1 and SSP3 scenarios for the period 2081-2100.
Abstract:
Earth system models underpin climate projections, but their cost limits how many scenarios, ensemble members and impact pathways can be explored. Machine-learning weather models forecast as skillfully as physics-based models at a fraction of the cost, yet coupled to an ocean they have produced weak El Nino variability or have learned it from climate-model output, and none carries the climate through rivers to the people exposed to it. Here we present Rescene-ESM, an Earth system model whose coupler, YAHO, yokes a frozen 20.8-million-parameter AI weather model to physically formulated ocean, sea-ice, land, river, carbon and energy components constrained by observations. Adding the couplings one at a time shows what each contributes: over a climatological ocean the AI atmosphere varies from year to year by a third of the observed amount over the oceans, and an interactive mixed layer, an equatorial wave ocean, the residual of the observed sea surface temperature (SST) equation as stochastic forcing and the land's moisture flux raise this step by step and bring El Nino and its teleconnections, leaving the mean climate almost unchanged. The full model simulates about 1,000 years per day on one graphics processing unit, holds the observed climate for a thousand years without drift, reproduces the observed 1979-2022 global warming, carries forced change with a physically based structure around the frozen network (transient climate response 1.60 K), routes runoff to 212 river gauges with a median seasonal-cycle correlation of 0.79 and, driven by emissions, separates the heatwave exposure of an SSP1 and an SSP3 world by a factor of 4.7 in 2081-2100, three-quarters of it through the warmer climate.

Abstract:
Scientific agents must connect research problems, methods, and findings dispersed through narrative articles. We introduce SPRA (Structured Paper Representation for Agents), a paper-level representation of persistent research objects and typed relations. Its empirical-AI profile organizes Problems, Contributions, Components, ExperimentSetups, Measures, and Findings for querying, traversal, and reuse. Selected paper-level citation relations record dependencies, component use, and comparisons with prior work; source locations support verification. We instantiate SPRA across more than one million AI papers, with 19.95 million research units and 24.07 million internal relations. Evaluations access SPRA through Schema-RAG with task evidence fixed before inference. Across seven subfields, SPRA improves source-conditioned scientific QA by 0.381/5 over passage retrieval while remaining close to Full Compact (0.078/5 lower on average). In directed cross-paper reasoning, qualification improves by 0.184/5 over passage retrieval; Top-5 synthesis shows mixed quality effects. Across all paired RQ2 tasks, including Top-5, input+output tokens decrease by 28.5% relative to passage retrieval and 95.3% relative to Full Compact. In idea generation, a 0.067-point lower Overall score than Full Compact accompanies 93.7% fewer input+output tokens. Together, these results support SPRA as a usable representation of scientific content and a foundation for research workflows that connect, inspect, and reuse paper-level research objects. The SPRA resource is accessible through the SciVerse scholarly-data platform.

Abstract:
The paper discusses the significance and advantages of an onomasiological approach to word formation. Abstract:
OBJECTIVE: Recent studies have raised concerns arising from the exploitation ("mining") of public health databases for low-quality, mass-produced papers. However, it remains challenging to disambiguate whether such papers originate from paper mills (commercial entities that sell authorships on mass-produced papers) or from the uncoordinated action of individuals facilitated by AI tools and templated workflows. Our study aims to address this question for one particular database, the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD). We selected this database after noticing that one of our papers on Bayesian age-period-cohort models has recently been experiencing a rapid surge in geographically clustered citations from GBD papers with Chinese affiliations. METHODS: We collected bibliometric and article-level metadata from GBD papers to search for indicators of mass-produced research. Moreover, we assessed 713 full-text articles for reported R versions, availability of code and data, and declaration of generative AI use. For 180 articles, we qualitatively screened the figures for graphical similarities. Finally, we conducted an exploratory scoping investigation of online platforms (social media sites, vendor websites) dedicated to do-it-yourself workflows for secondary analyses of public health data. RESULTS: Although we cannot rule out paper mill involvement, our findings suggest that the geographically clustered increase in GBD publications from China is at least partially driven by independent authors. CONCLUSION: Appropriate efforts should be directed towards evaluating the quality of the identified workflows. Stakeholders in scientific integrity should monitor online platforms dedicated to the rapid production of papers, especially in light of the increasing focus on AI-assisted workflows. Code sharing should be mandated for data-driven secondary analyses. Paywalls and proprietary software licenses hinder transparency and reusability, underscoring the importance of free and open-source software for trustworthy and reproducible research.
