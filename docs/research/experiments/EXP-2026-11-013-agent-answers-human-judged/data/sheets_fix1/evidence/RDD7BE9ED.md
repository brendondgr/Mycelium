Setting (Mycelium's reading): Mediterranean coralligenous reefs, utilizing a historical record of mass mortality events from 1986 to 2020.
Abstract:
Accelerating marine heatwaves are outpacing static conservation strategies, threatening the functional integrity of temperate reefs globally. Implementing “climate-smart” Marine Spatial Planning requires moving beyond retrospective impact assessment toward prospective tools that can integrate local context to anticipate vulnerability. This study presents an explainable, policy-aware early warning system that leverages the most comprehensive historical record of mass mortality events (1986-2020) to forecast site-level risk in the Mediterranean coralligenous reefs. Using a rigorous modelling framework validated on independent prospective test data (2019-2020), the model achieves high forecasting skill (average precision = 0.75, where 1.0 is perfect and the baseline is 0.70) and maintains temporal robustness by simulating real-world forecasting across the historical record. Model interpretability indicates that macroclimatic thermal stress is modulated by local factors such as depth and spatial protection. For instance, each decade since marine protected area (MPA) designation corresponds to an estimated ~48% decrease in the odds of elevated mortality (95% CI: 17–72%) under high heat stress, although this modulating signal was statistically detectable primarily in the Western Mediterranean and does not imply absolute resistance against extreme thermal anomalies. By translating these complex, context-dependent predictions into equitable, subregion-balanced alerts through an open-access interactive decision-support platform, this study provides a practical forecasting tool blueprint to optimize monitoring surveys, guide proactive triage, and support climate-adaptive Marine Spatial Planning under accelerating ocean warming.

### Evidence #5 [paper] Rethinking Marine Heatwaves: Towards a Probabilistic Marine Heat Stress
Authors: Natalija Dunić, Krešimir Ruić, Ivica Vilibić
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.22541/essoar.15011394/v1
Still open/current: yes
Problem (Mycelium's reading): Standard marine heatwave (MHW) definitions rely on statistical temperature anomalies relative to a climatological baseline, which does not directly account for the thermal exposure experienced by marine organisms or its biological consequences.
Method (Mycelium's reading): The Probabilistic Marine Heat Stress Wave (PMHSW) framework, which integrates temperature exceedance above a biological threshold across time and depth to estimate cumulative thermal exposure and relates this exposure to the probability of a defined biological response.
Setting (Mycelium's reading): Synthetic examples used to demonstrate the estimation of response functions from biological observations and the derivation of binary event definitions.
Abstract:
Marine heatwaves (MHWs) are commonly defined as statistically extreme temperature events relative to a climatological baseline. While this approach provides a consistent description of ocean temperature extremes, it does not directly account for the thermal exposure experienced by marine organisms or its biological consequences. Here, we propose the Probabilistic Marine Heat Stress Wave (PMHSW) framework, which shifts the focus from temperature anomalies toward biologically relevant thermal exposure. The framework integrates temperature exceedance above a biological threshold across time and depth to estimate cumulative thermal exposure and relates this exposure to the probability of a defined biological response. The probabilistic formulation allows differences among organisms, habitats and thermal tolerances to be represented while retaining a continuous description of biological heat stress. Using synthetic examples, we demonstrate how the response function can be estimated from biological observations and how a binary event definition can be obtained when needed. The PMHSW framework is intended as a complementary approach to existing MHW definitions, particularly when the objective is to assess the potential biological consequences of marine thermal extremes under present and future climate conditions.

### Evidence #6 [paper] The evolution of the Dutch heat early warning system (2007 - 2025)

Venue: Climate Services
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cliser.2026.100718
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Heat early warning systems (HEWS) are critical tools for anticipating hazardous heat and enabling timely response. While some of the earliest systems were developed over two decades ago, little is known about how and why systems have evolved in practice. This study therefore examines the evolution of the Dutch heat warning system from 2007 to 2025 and evaluates the future performance of current warning criteria. We analysed 18 operational documents, including seven post-event evaluations, to identify operational challenges and drivers of change. Current warning criteria were also applied to KNMI’23 climate scenarios to assess future warning frequency, duration, intensity, and season length. Future projections indicate a substantial increase in heat warnings, from ~2 per year currently to 3.9–7 by 2050 and up to 29 by 2100. The findings provide lessons for meteorological and public health agencies by showing how operational, institutional, climate, and scientific factors shape the design of heat early warning systems.

Venue: SOLA
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.1007/s44393-026-00038-8
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Abstract Extreme high temperature and humidity threaten high-latitude regions that are less adapted to such conditions. While marine heatwaves (MHWs) can contribute to hot and humid conditions over land, the modulation of these impacts by the day-to-day atmospheric circulation remains poorly understood. This study focused on Hokkaido, the northernmost prefecture in Japan, to investigate how the daily atmospheric circulation regulated the distribution and intensity of MHW-related heatstroke risk during August 2023. Our findings suggest that the relative positioning of airflow pathways and the SST distribution should be considered to improve heatstroke risk prediction in coastal regions.

Abstract. This study presents cSINGV v1.0, a high-resolution (1.5 km) regional coupled atmosphere–ocean–wave modelling system developed for the Western Maritime Continent (WMC). Method (Mycelium's reading): Analysis of Community Earth System Model version 2 simulations, ocean-assimilated experiments, and five-year hindcasts initialized annually from 1981 to 2020 to assess MHW frequency, cumulative intensity, and duration.
Setting (Mycelium's reading): 12 vulnerable coral reef regions across various ocean basins, using historical data from 1981 to 2020.
Abstract:
Marine heat waves (MHWs) increasingly threaten coral reefs, but their predictability beyond seasonal timescales remains poorly understood. Here we assess the seasonal-to-multi-year predictability of MHW frequency, cumulative intensity, and duration across 12 coral reef regions. Using Community Earth System Model version 2 simulations, ocean-assimilated experiments and five-year hindcasts initialized annually from 1981 to 2020, we show that frequency and cumulative intensity are predictable for several months to multiple years across many ocean basins, with skill varying strongly by region and season. Anthropogenic warming is the dominant source of multi-year skill across most hotspots, while internal variability limits predictability in the equatorial Pacific. El Niño-Southern Oscillation and Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation further modulate regional forecasts through coupled ocean-atmosphere processes. These findings reveal strong regional contrasts in the sources and limits of forecast skill, identifying where advance warning can support timely, targeted regional preparedness, coral reef conservation and adaptation to escalating thermal stress. The study shows that marine heatwave frequency and cumulative intensity across vulnerable coral reef regions are skillfully predictable from months to years ahead, with long-term anthropogenic warming accounting for most multi-year forecast skill, while internal variability, particularly El Niño, limits predictability in the equatorial Pacific.

### Evidence #19 [paper] Multi-season evaluation of temperature and wind in the marine boundary layer along the United States northeast coast in the High-Resolution Rapid Refresh model
Authors: Bianca Adler, Laura Bianco, David D. Turner, Joseph Olson, Xia Sun, Joshua Gebauer, Nicola Bodini, Stefano Letizia
Venue: Geoscientific model development
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.5194/gmd-19-8627-2026
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
The High-Resolution Rapid Refresh (HRRR) model is run operationally by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration to provide high-resolution short-range forecasts for the continental United States. The evaluation of the HRRR model off of the United States coasts has been challenged by the lack of suitable continuous profile observations in the marine boundary layer in the past. State-of-the art remote sensing instruments were recently deployed along the coast of New England in the northeastern United States for the multi-year Third Wind Forecast Improvement Project and provide a unique opportunity for the evaluation of temperature and wind in the marine boundary layer in the HRRR model. We used 1 year of data at three sites, two of which were on islands, to document the seasonal characteristics of the marine boundary layer and its representation in the HRRR model for different forecast hours. Overall, the HRRR model captured the seasonal and diurnal evolution of temperature and wind very well. However, low-level horizontal wind shear and static stability were too weak in the model, especially during the warmer months. At certain sites, these biases were accompanied by errors in sea surface temperature, suggesting a potential, though localized, link. Low-level jets (LLJs) occurred in approximately 20 % of the hourly profiles with a maximum frequency during spring and summer. Up to 60 % of the LLJ profiles during peak seasons were correctly predicted, using the critical success index as a measure. Systematic model errors in wind and temperature were found during LLJs, when the HRRR model frequently underestimated wind speed at nose height and shear below nose height, often accompanied by static stability that was too weak. These errors resulted in low-level Bulk Richardson numbers that were consistently too large at all three sites, indicating an overestimation of dynamic stability in the boundary layer in the model. Such systematic errors in low-level wind shear and stability were largely absent during correct rejections, that is, when an LLJ was neither observed nor simulated, indicating that LLJs were responsible for a large part of the model errors.

Setting (Mycelium's reading): Significant wave height data from two Australian sites (Gladstone and Palm Beach) from 2018 to 2022, with forecast horizons ranging from 30 minutes to 24 hours.
Abstract:
Accurate forecasting of significant wave height (Hs) is vital for marine safety, coastal engineering, and renewable energy production. This study presents an effective integrated model system to forecast Hs for two distinct sites in Australia, Gladstone and Palm Beach, over various time horizons from 30-min to 24-h ahead during the period of 2018 to 2022. In the first stage, a novel multi-objective Optuna is developed to optimize the decomposition parameters using two criteria of compactness, bandwidth, and peakedness of a signal's frequency spectrum. The proposed methodology is utilized for hyperparameter tuning of Singular Spectrum Analysis (SSA) and De-mixing Multivariate Variational Mode Decomposition (D-MVMD) techniques. This enables an optimal data decomposition process by eliminating the need for manual parameter selection. In the second stage, an explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) approach, SHAPley Additive Explanations (SHAP), is used to identify the most influential components and analyze their interactions. These optimized and interpretable decomposed signals are then used as inputs to standalone forecasting models, including Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), deep Random Vector Functional Link (RVFL), and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). These optimized and interpretable decomposed signals are then used as inputs to the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), deep Random Vector Functional Link (RVFL), and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) models to forecast. In the final stage, a comprehensive Monte Carlo Simulation (MCS) is employed to quantify uncertainties and perform reliability analysis. The SHAP-based feature selection reduced the input variables from 37 to 48 decomposed components to a single dominant predictor while maintaining nearly identical predictive accuracy across all forecast horizons, significantly lowering computational costs. Among the models, the SSA-RVFL demonstrated superior performance, with lower computational cost and the highest forecasting accuracy across all time horizons. This model achieved the highest Index of Agreement (IA: 0.81-1) and determination coefficient (R 2 : 0.49-0.98), and lowest Root Mean Square Error (RMSE: 0.04-0.33) across stations and horizons. In addition, the results confirm operational reliability exceeding 88% for forecasts up to 6 h ahead. Overall, the fully optimized SSA integrated with the SHAP-RVFL-MCS results provides a robust framework for enhancing decision-making in coastal hazard and wave energy planning.

### Evidence #21 [paper] Deeper Structure of Marine Heatwaves: Glider-Detected Anomalies in the North Sea
Authors: Charlotte Z. Reed, Victor-Alexandru Darvariu, Nick Hawes, Helen Louise Johnson
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.22541/essoar.15009434/v1
Still open/current: yes
Problem (Mycelium's reading): Satellite-derived sea surface temperatures cannot always be used to estimate the spatial structure, duration, intensity, and drivers for temperatures below the ocean surface.
Method (Mycelium's reading): Comparison of near-continuous autonomous underwater glider observations with GLORYS12V1 ocean reanalysis and regional heat content analysis.
Setting (Mycelium's reading): North Sea, specifically off the East coast of Scotland and the area between the Shetland and Orkney Islands.
Abstract:
Marine heatwaves are often characterized by sea surface temperatures derived from satellite data. However, surface anomalies cannot always be used to estimate the spatial structure, duration, intensity, and drivers for temperatures below the surface. In this study, we use near-continuous autonomous underwater glider observations from a deployment off the East coast of Scotland, accompanied by the GLORYS12V1 ocean reanalysis, to examine recent high temperature anomalies in the North Sea. Glider observations show that warm and cold anomalies can be stronger below the surface than what is represented in the reanalysis, and discrepancies are often associated with differences in the timing and depth of mixed layer depth changes. Regional heat content analysis indicates that anomalies within the mixed layer are strongly influenced by surface heat fluxes, while anomalies below are more closely associated with advective heat transport, particularly inflow from the North Atlantic between the Shetland and Orkney Islands. A prolonged anomaly in 2024-2026 persisted below the surface across multiple seasons, suggesting that subsurface heat storage may hold memory between surface events. Our results demonstrate that marine heatwaves in shelf seas can have substantial subsurface structure, even in a relatively shallow and seasonally well-mixed region. Sustained glider observations can provide key observations alongside satellite and reanalysis products, improving analysis and validation of subsurface temperature anomalies in shelf seas.
