Abstract:
INTRODUCTION: Patients with musculoskeletal complaints often search online to identify an appropriate healthcare provider. With the increasing availability of large language models (LLMs), these artificial intelligence (AI) tools can direct patients to providers. This study evaluated the ability of LLMs to recommend appropriate providers based on representative patient musculoskeletal queries. METHODS: Three LLMs (ChatGPT, DeepSeek, and Gemini) were prompted with standardized musculoskeletal queries for two US cities (Lynchburg, VA, and Trumbull, CT). Provider recommendations were considered appropriate if the physician was currently practicing in the requested location and specialized in the relevant area. Listed phone numbers were checked for accuracy. Descriptive statistics and Fisher exact tests were used to summarize findings. DISCUSSION: LLMs showed potential to direct patients to local, specialized musculoskeletal providers based on their report, although the specific contact information was at times inaccurate. As these tools evolve, providers should be aware of AI's ability to make provider recommendations and work to ensure the presentation of their contact information is accessible by these models as best possible.

Authors: Henriette Lorenzen, Diana Mejlstrup Barrett Momsen, Lene Gredal, Marianne Ellegaard
Venue: Medical Science Educator
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.1007/s40670-026-02930-1
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Abstract Introduction Clinical placements, where students learn through participation in everyday work, are a central component of many health education programs, including biomedical laboratory science (BLS). While research has primarily focused on students’ experiences, less is known about how participating staff perceive their role. This study explores how BLS staff perceive and enact clinical training. Materials and methods A qualitative study was conducted comprising four focus groups with BLS staff from two hospital settings, including both experienced and newly employed staff. Interviews were analysed using inductive thematic analysis. Activity theory guided a subsequent analysis of workplace learning tensions. Strengthening clinical education requires recognising training as a shared organisational responsibility and creating conditions that support student participation.

Abstract:
Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly being applied in clinical laboratories to support laboratory processes and interpretation of test results. Recent developments in machine learning, deep learning, and large language models have expanded the potential applications of AI across different stages of laboratory testing. However, the use of AI in clinical laboratories also raises important questions regarding validation, analytical performance, and transferability between laboratories. This narrative review examines recent applications of AI across the total testing process in clinical chemistry. Recent English-language scientific literature published from 2023 to 18 August 2026 was identified through searches of PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar. It describes AI-assisted test utilization; specimen handling and quality assessment; detection of laboratory errors; patient-based quality control; interpretation of complex analytical outputs, including signals from emerging AI-integrated biosensors and nanomaterial-based analytical platforms; result verification; and laboratory-report interpretation. The review also discusses major implementation challenges, including analytical variability, data quality, sensor and platform reproducibility, model generalizability, interoperability, and limitations of large language models. Particular attention is given to external validation, transportability, and real-world implementation. Among the studies considered in this review, many applications remain supported mainly by retrospective or internally validated studies, while prospective and operational evaluations are less common. Finally, practical considerations for validation, local verification, quality assurance, and performance monitoring are discussed. AI has considerable potential to support clinical chemistry laboratories, but safe use requires appropriate evaluation and continued involvement of laboratory professionals.

Abstract:
BACKGROUND: Artificial Intelligence (AI) has grown quickly in healthcare and has had a big effect on medical laboratory diagnostics, especially when it comes to diagnosing blood disorders. To figure out the pros and cons of using AI in hematology diagnostics, it is important to understand the perspective of medical laboratory workers instead of specialists toward AI use in hematology laboratory diagnostics. The goal of this study was to investigate what medical lab workers now know about AI and how they think it affects the accuracy of diagnostic hematology and patient outcomes. ### Evidence #13 [paper] Assigned roles change how clinical AI agents allocate shared resources
Authors: A. Gorenshtein, M. Omar, Y. Barash, J. B. Kruskal, M. Ahmed, O. Brook, E. Klang
Venue: medRxiv
Year: 2026
Link: https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/2e795227d001fc2dbe836d8700e35fc3bfdd8760
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Clinical AI agents may be assigned to individual patients, but hospital resources are shared across many patients. We tested what agents do when helping their assigned patient would violate the hospital's rule for a scarce resource. We analyzed 22,916 simulated cases comprising 274,992 logged agent actions across 20 AI models. In each scenario, the agent could claim a scarce resource for its patient even though the hospital rule gave another patient priority. We varied only the agent's assigned role, from responsibility for the whole ward to strong advocacy for one patient. Violations of the hospital rule rose from 32.5% under whole-ward responsibility to 69.4% under strong patient advocacy, a 36.9-point increase (95% CI, 25.7-48.0). Agents correctly identified which patient should receive the resource in 95.7% of tests, yet still took it for their own patient in 65.9% of those episodes. Asking the agent to apply its own allocation judgment immediately before acting reduced violations to 0-2% in a three-model follow-up experiment. Assigned roles can shape how clinical AI agents use shared hospital resources, even when they identify the correct priority patient. Patient-focused agents should not independently control shared resources without an allocation check.

### Evidence #14 [paper] Knowledge and self-reported practice of laboratory biosafety among medical laboratory professionals in selected public and private hospitals in Mogadishu, Somalia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Omar Sidow Zubair, Ahmed Abdi Yonis, Bashir Abukar Mohamud, Mohamed Hassan Mohamud
Venue: Frontiers in Public Health
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1916020
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Background Laboratory biosafety protects healthcare workers, patients and the environment, yet evidence from fragile and resource-limited settings remains scarce. In Somalia, previous work has assessed infection prevention and control at the facility level, but the biosafety knowledge and practice of laboratory professionals themselves, and the relationship between the two, have not been characterized. This study assessed knowledge and self-reported practice of laboratory biosafety, and factors associated with good practice, among medical laboratory professionals in public and private hospitals in Mogadishu, Somalia. Methods A facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 164 eligible medical laboratory professionals in 12 hospitals selected by stratified random sampling from the public and private hospital sectors in Mogadishu. All eligible professionals within the selected hospitals were enumerated; the study is therefore a complete enumeration within sampled facilities rather than a census of all laboratory professionals in Mogadishu. Data were collected with a structured, self-administered questionnaire adapted from the WHO Laboratory Biosafety Manual and previously validated knowledge–practice instruments. Knowledge was measured with 8 dichotomously scored items (range 0–8; KR-20 = 0.60) and self-reported practice with 9 dichotomous items (range 0–9; KR-20 = 0.52), both classified using pre-specified modified Bloom’s cut-off points. Distributions were assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Associations were examined using the Pearson chi-square test and Spearman’s rank correlation, and factors associated with good practice using univariable and multivariable binary logistic regression. Results The median knowledge score was 7 (IQR 5–7) of 8 and the median practice score 9 (IQR 8–9) of 9; both departed significantly from normality. Knowledge was high for hand hygiene (89.0%) and personal protective equipment (86.6%) but low for infectious waste colour-coding (53.7%). Overall, 83 participants (50.6%) had good knowledge, 51 (31.1%) moderate and 30 (18.3%) poor. Self-reported practice was high for lab coat use (98.2%), hand hygiene (98.2%) and glove use (97.6%), and lower for equipment inspection (78.7%) and accident reporting (81.7%); 134 participants (81.7%) were classified as having good practice. A statistically significant positive association was observed between knowledge level and practice level ( χ 2 = 19.58, df = 2, p < 0.001; Cramer’s V = 0.35; Spearman’s ρ = 0.296, p < 0.001), with good practice reported by 94.0, 74.5 and 60.0% of those with good, moderate and poor knowledge, respectively. In multivariable analysis, each additional correct knowledge answer independently increased the odds of good practice (aOR 1.73, 95% CI 1.33–2.24, p < 0.001), as did participation in a safety drill in the preceding year (aOR 2.80, 95% CI 1.09–7.15, p = 0.032). Only 64.6% of participants had taken part in such a drill. Conclusion Medical laboratory professionals in Mogadishu hospitals demonstrated moderate biosafety knowledge and high self-reported biosafety practice, although there were gaps in knowledge of infectious waste colour-coding. Knowledge scores were positively associated with self-reported practice, while recent participation in a safety drill was independently associated with reported participants’ practice. These findings suggest that regular safety drills and targeted reinforcement of infectious waste-management procedures may be useful areas for strengthening biosafety practices. However, the high reported practice scores and their potential effect underscore the need for future studies to assess biosafety practices through direct observation.

### Evidence #17 [paper] ExpertTwins: Auditable Long Horizon Scientific Panels through Grounded Expertise
Authors: Ali Foroughi pour, Abbas Shirinifard
Venue: bioRxiv (Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory)
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.10.06.757114
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Multi-agent language-model systems increasingly assign roles to agents and let them deliberate. A role label, however, does not specify which evidence an agent can bring or which standard it applies before accepting a conclusion. Agents that read one another's outputs tend to converge. We present ExpertTwins, a framework in which each seat of a scientific panel is defined by an inspectable evidence policy. Discipline seats retrieve under field-specific queries, and scientist-associated seats retrieve from attributed publications and a citation-derived scholarly neighborhood. Private initial packets, structured exchange, cited-source disclosure, deterministic quotation checks, and peer source review turn deliberation into an auditable record, which we also render as interactive idea-flow maps. Scientist associations denote publication-grounded perspectives, not replicas or endorsements of named researchers. In a nine-seat, four-turn neuroblastoma panel, 438 of 439 claims passed all attached quotation checks, and seats withdrew or defended positions when challenged on their evidence. A ten-turn panel sustained evidence acquisition and differentiated recommendations without conversational collapse. Across six base models given byte-identical initial packets, four sustained 97.9-99.8% quotation grounding, and at least 91% on every turn, whereas Haiku 4.5 failed by paraphrase and GPT-5 mini by silence. Agreement among strong models fell from 31% of claims at turn one to 5% at turn four. With follow-up questions held fixed, it stayed at 28-33% through turn three and fell to 20% only at the closing turn. Stress runs showed the collapse guardrail escalating through its full intervention ladder when consensus was forced, but missing deference to one unusually strong seat. On idea-flow maps, the forced consensus appeared as parallel lanes of identical ideas, although quotation grounding and participation balance stayed at their maxima. We also reimplemented two close published systems and ran them on matched models, seats, and questions. A multi-persona debate cited only real records, yet none of its cited sentences carried a checkable quotation and it had no way to abstain. An author-memory fusion system ran only after its fact checker was split by seat to fit in context. Its fused answer kept about two-thirds of the fact-checked specialist claims, answered 36 of 60 question parts on which our seats had abstained, and showed the reader 19% of its agents' disagreements. In a computational pathology case study, six scientist-associated agents built distinct pipelines to predict MYCN amplification from whole-slide images. Across six base models, seat-by-model interaction explained 57% of internal variance, and internal rank poorly predicted external rank (r = 0.18 across 36 nominees). These results establish differentiated, source-traceable scientific reasoning as an operational capability and identify the base model as a parameter that must be reported with the protocol.

Abstract The SingHealth Duke–NUS Academic Medical Center manages over 2,800 clinical faculty members and processes over 400 appointments and promotions annually. ### Evidence #21 [paper] Perceptions of ChatGPT- and veterinarian-generated responses to pet owners’ medical questions
Authors: Aïnoha Griot, Rob Ward
Venue: Frontiers in Veterinary Science
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2026.1939644
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Background Artificial Intelligence (AI) is becoming increasingly significant in veterinary medicine, particularly in answering pet owners’ medical questions. AI’s integration could reduce veterinary professionals’ workload while improving access to medical advice. However, the reliability and quality of AI-generated responses compared to veterinarians’ remain unexplored. The objective was to determine whether AI-generated responses were perceived as comparable to veterinarian-generated responses. Methods This cross-sectional study analysed the answers to a sample of pet owners’ medical questions. Both a veterinarian and ChatGPT (GPT-4o, May 2024) provided answers, which were anonymised and presented in a survey distributed through mailing lists and public Facebook groups. Participants were asked to assess each response based on structure, quality, and empathy, using a five-point Likert scale. They then had to indicate whether they trusted the response, and which response they preferred. Results Over two-thirds of the 201 participants (68%) preferred the ChatGPT-generated answers ( p < 0.05), which were rated higher in structure, quality, and empathy. Limitations This study has several limitations, including a small sample size, reliance on a single veterinarian, and use of only two Reddit-derived scenarios. Response accuracy was not assessed, and word count restrictions may have affected perceived response quality. Additionally, prior research suggests that irrelevant patient details can impact ChatGPT’s accuracy, raising questions about user trust if clinical inaccuracies were present. Conclusion AI-generated responses received higher participant ratings in structure, quality and empathy, suggesting AI may enhance veterinary client communication when used under appropriate professional supervision.
