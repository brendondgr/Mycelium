Abstract:
The increasing complexity of artificial intelligence (AI) systems, scientific computing environments, data analytics platforms, and automated computational workflows has created substantial opportunities for reducing redundant computation through the anticipation of future computational requirements. Modern computational tasks are frequently organized as interconnected workflows consisting of multiple dependent operations, including data acquisition, preprocessing, transformation, feature extraction, model execution, intermediate result generation, and final output production. Although existing workflow optimization techniques investigate caching, memoization, intermediate result reuse, and dependency-aware scheduling, computational preparation is predominantly associated with previously executed workflows or workloads that have already been submitted. This paper introduces Predictive Computational Graph Preparation (PCGP), a conceptual framework that investigates whether future computational workflows can be forecast and their reusable intermediate computational nodes selectively executed before actual workflow requests materialize. Unlike predictive inference preparation, which primarily anticipates future questions or responses, PCGP focuses on predicting the computational structures underlying future tasks. Its architecture integrates future workflow prediction, computational graph construction, shared subgraph identification, node-level demand forecasting, dependency-aware speculative execution, intermediate result preservation, compatibility verification, and adaptive workflow reconstruction. A central objective is to identify computational nodes that are likely to participate in multiple future workflows and prepare their outputs in advance, thereby reducing repeated execution across different workflows rather than merely accelerating repeated instances of an identical task. The framework further investigates economic-aware node preparation, allowing eligible computations to be scheduled during periods of relatively low GPU utilization, favorable electricity prices, or lower-cost cloud resource availability. A unified optimization formulation is developed to evaluate the expected benefit of precomputing individual nodes and shared subgraphs while accounting for prediction uncertainty, dependency constraints, execution expenditure, storage requirements, data freshness, result compatibility, and resource availability. Importantly, the framework distinguishes between historical intermediate result caching, predictive node execution, and predictive subgraph preparation, recognizing that these mechanisms provide different forms of computational reuse. An experimental methodology is proposed to evaluate workflow prediction accuracy, node reuse rates, avoided computational expenditure, execution latency, GPU utilization, energy consumption, and total system cost. The proposed framework does not assume that every predicted node should be executed in advance; its effectiveness depends on the availability of valid inputs, workflow predictability, cross-workflow reuse opportunities, preparation expenditure, and the compatibility of prepared results with subsequently submitted workflows. By shifting the optimization target from individual future requests to the computational structures underlying future workflows, this research establishes a conceptual foundation for predictive computation graph preparation across data analytics, scientific computing, machine learning pipelines, and AI agent systems. Its practical effectiveness remains subject to empirical validation.

The three major gaps that have been identified from a structured review of literature involving 50 papers and thematic analysis of eight stakeholders including Principal Investigators, Post-Doctoral Researchers, Lab Managers, Computational Scientists, Procurement Officers, Facility Engineers, Industry R&D Managers, and Sustainability Officers are: (1) disconnect between computational screening and procurement process, (2) lack of predictive lead-time models for laboratory chemicals, and (3) lack of closed-loop approach for R&D at circular economy. The quantitative results show that the average score for predictability of procurement lead-time is 2.0/5, whereas the average score for severity of delays is 4.4/5. The average recovery of precursors' mass is 26%. There are four main qualitative themes: "Wait-and-see Trap", "Customs and Bureaucracy Black Box", "Cost vs. Purity Dilemma", and "Desperately Predictive Need". SynchroLabs' SCM Framework consists of four components: Predictive Procurement Engine, Lead-Time Intelligence Dashboard, Closed-Loop Recovery Engine, and Integrated Smart Inventory. Readiness for adoption was reported to be 4.4/5 on average, demonstrating high potential for implementation. Contribution to the theory of supply chain is that it applies the theory of closed-loop to laboratories and offers recommendations for laboratory managers, institution administrators, and developers of the technologies of synthesis and computational discovery.
