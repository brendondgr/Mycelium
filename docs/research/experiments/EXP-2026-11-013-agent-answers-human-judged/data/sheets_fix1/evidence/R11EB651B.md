### Evidence #1 [paper] Rethinking Marine Heatwaves: Towards a Probabilistic Marine Heat Stress
Authors: Natalija Dunić, Krešimir Ruić, Ivica Vilibić
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.22541/essoar.15011394/v1
Still open/current: yes
Problem (Mycelium's reading): Standard marine heatwave (MHW) definitions rely on statistical temperature anomalies relative to a climatological baseline, which does not directly account for the thermal exposure experienced by marine organisms or its biological consequences.
Method (Mycelium's reading): The Probabilistic Marine Heat Stress Wave (PMHSW) framework, which integrates temperature exceedance above a biological threshold across time and depth to estimate cumulative thermal exposure and relates this exposure to the probability of a defined biological response.
Setting (Mycelium's reading): Synthetic examples used to demonstrate the estimation of response functions from biological observations and the derivation of binary event definitions.
Abstract:
Marine heatwaves (MHWs) are commonly defined as statistically extreme temperature events relative to a climatological baseline. While this approach provides a consistent description of ocean temperature extremes, it does not directly account for the thermal exposure experienced by marine organisms or its biological consequences. Here, we propose the Probabilistic Marine Heat Stress Wave (PMHSW) framework, which shifts the focus from temperature anomalies toward biologically relevant thermal exposure. The framework integrates temperature exceedance above a biological threshold across time and depth to estimate cumulative thermal exposure and relates this exposure to the probability of a defined biological response. The probabilistic formulation allows differences among organisms, habitats and thermal tolerances to be represented while retaining a continuous description of biological heat stress. Using synthetic examples, we demonstrate how the response function can be estimated from biological observations and how a binary event definition can be obtained when needed. The PMHSW framework is intended as a complementary approach to existing MHW definitions, particularly when the objective is to assess the potential biological consequences of marine thermal extremes under present and future climate conditions.

Abstract:
Accelerating marine heatwaves are outpacing static conservation strategies, threatening the functional integrity of temperate reefs globally. Implementing “climate-smart” Marine Spatial Planning requires moving beyond retrospective impact assessment toward prospective tools that can integrate local context to anticipate vulnerability. This study presents an explainable, policy-aware early warning system that leverages the most comprehensive historical record of mass mortality events (1986-2020) to forecast site-level risk in the Mediterranean coralligenous reefs. Using a rigorous modelling framework validated on independent prospective test data (2019-2020), the model achieves high forecasting skill (average precision = 0.75, where 1.0 is perfect and the baseline is 0.70) and maintains temporal robustness by simulating real-world forecasting across the historical record. Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.31223/x5kv4t
Still open/current: yes
Abstract:
Marine heatwaves (MHWs) negatively impact marine ecosystems and coastal economies. Here, we evaluate whether a preemptive, short-term, localized surface emission of sea salt aerosols (marine cloud brightening, MCB) can mitigate the impacts of a predicted MHW without creating significant effects beyond the intervention zone. As a case study, we apply MCB to the 2019 Northeast Pacific MHW, "The Blob 2.0," using a global climate model seasonal-to-multiyear predictability ensemble. Abstract:
Introduction Marine heatwaves (MHWs) are an increasing climate hazard for coastal aquaculture, yet regional assessments may not resolve how exposure varies across the locations where farms operate. Method (Mycelium's reading): Analysis of Community Earth System Model version 2 simulations, ocean-assimilated experiments, and five-year hindcasts initialized annually from 1981 to 2020 to assess MHW frequency, cumulative intensity, and duration.

Abstract:
Marine heat waves (MHWs) increasingly threaten coral reefs, but their predictability beyond seasonal timescales remains poorly understood. Here we assess the seasonal-to-multi-year predictability of MHW frequency, cumulative intensity, and duration across 12 coral reef regions. Using Community Earth System Model version 2 simulations, ocean-assimilated experiments and five-year hindcasts initialized annually from 1981 to 2020, we show that frequency and cumulative intensity are predictable for several months to multiple years across many ocean basins, with skill varying strongly by region and season. These findings reveal strong regional contrasts in the sources and limits of forecast skill, identifying where advance warning can support timely, targeted regional preparedness, coral reef conservation and adaptation to escalating thermal stress. The study shows that marine heatwave frequency and cumulative intensity across vulnerable coral reef regions are skillfully predictable from months to years ahead, with long-term anthropogenic warming accounting for most multi-year forecast skill, while internal variability, particularly El Niño, limits predictability in the equatorial Pacific.

Here, we characterise properties such as the intensity, duration, and heterogeneity of subsurface MHWs using the Copernicus Global Ocean Reanalysis Ensemble Product (GREP). MHWs are detected across multiple depths (0.5–200 m) using a consistent percentile-based thresholding, applied to three reanalysis members (CGLORSv7, GLORYS2v4, ORAS5) and evaluated against well‑documented historical events. All reanalyses successfully capture the timing and general evolution of major, well-known surface expressions of MHW events when compared against the ESA Climate Change Initiative SST dataset in given areas of interest, such as, for example, events of 2011 in Western Australia, of 2015 in the Northeast Pacific Blob, and of 2006 in the Mediterranean Sea. Here, it is also shown that anomalies frequently penetrate below the mixed layer, down to 100–150 m. On a global scale, there is a positive bias of about 15 days in the mean duration for all the events with respect to satellite SST data, consistent with the limited eddy-resolving capability of the eddy-permitting GREP reanalyses, the additional smoothing introduced by ensemble averaging and the difference between the different representative depths of SST in satellite observations and models. Global diagnostics reveal that MHWs extending into the subsurface are more persistent, spatially heterogeneous, and characterised by sharper onset and decline rates than shallow events, particularly in the tropics, western boundary currents, and the Southern Ocean. Mixed‑layer variability strongly modulates the vertical propagation of anomalies, underscoring the role of vertical mixing in shaping subsurface extremes. Our results highlight both the value and limitations of current reanalyses for subsurface MHW characterisation, emphasising the need for ensemble‑based approaches to quantify uncertainty.

Abstract:
The Great Barrier Reef (GBR) is increasingly threatened by marine heatwaves and mass coral bleaching events, with local atmospheric conditions playing a critical role in modulating sea surface temperatures and heat stress. This study examines how synoptic-to-mesoscale meteorological processes shaped cloud formation, precipitation and the ocean surface heat budget across the central GBR during the 2021–22 austral summer. Using a combination of observations, reanalysis data and high-resolution convection-permitting simulations, two representative cases are analysed: a trade wind breakdown period associated with early-summer heating and a trade wind regime linked to mid-summer cooling. The breakdown case featured enhanced convective available potential energy, deep convection, greater upper-level moisture transport and heavier rainfall, while exhibiting reduced latent heat flux owing to weaker winds. By contrast, the trade wind case was characterised by shallow clouds, dry atmospheric conditions, stronger latent cooling and minimal rainfall. Mesoscale convective systems during breakdown periods generated temporally limited but spatially extensive increases in surface winds and turbulent heat fluxes, particularly near the coast where coral reefs are concentrated. These episodes contributed to localised cooling of the ocean surface, offering temporary relief from heat stress, with this effect not being captured in coarser reanalyses. This study highlights the crucial role of synoptic-to-mesoscale atmospheric variability in influencing the surface heat budget of the GBR and demonstrates the added value of high-resolution simulations in resolving mesoscale processes relevant to reef-scale heat stress. These findings have important implications for assessing coral bleaching risk under current and future climate conditions.

Authors: Nikolaus Groll, Iris Grabemann
Venue: Ocean science
Year: 2026
Link: https://doi.org/10.5194/os-22-3185-2026
Still open/current: yes
Problem (Mycelium's reading): Assessing the potential impacts of future climate change on annual median and extreme wave conditions in the North Sea and the Baltic Sea.
Method (Mycelium's reading): Ensemble analysis of up to 14 regional projections for the North Sea and 8 for the Baltic Sea, based on IPCC CMIP3 and CMIP5 scenarios, incorporating various global and regional climate models, initial conditions, and emission scenarios.
Setting (Mycelium's reading): North Sea and Baltic Sea, using regional climate model projections towards the end of the 21st century.
Abstract:
Abstract. Storms and associated marine hazards pose a environmental risks, particularly under long-term changes driven by anthropogenic climate change. This study assesses the potential impacts of future climate change on annual median and extreme wave conditions in the North Sea and the Baltic Sea using a ensemble of up to 14 and 8 regional projections, respectively. The ensemble includes simulations based on IPCC CMIP3 and CMIP5 scenarios, incorporating a range of global and regional climate models, initial conditions, and emission scenarios to reflect diverse sources of uncertainty. High- and low-emission sub-ensembles were used to evaluate the influence of emission scenarios on projected wave climate changes towards the end of the 21st century. Despite variations in magnitude and spatial patterns across the ensemble, some robust spatial and temporal trends emerge. A meridional gradient of changes is simulated in the North Sea. Significant wave heights decrease along the western margins and increase in the east. These changes are more pronounced under high-emission scenarios, with median values decreasing by up to 5 % in the west and annual maxima increasing by over 5 % in the east. Time series analysis confirms these trends at selected locations, although there is still considerable internal variability among ensemble members. In the Baltic Sea, both median and extreme wave heights are simulated to increase by more than 5 % in some areas. Although the response to emission scenarios is less distinct than in the North Sea, high-emission scenarios still yield more pronounced increases. Temporal analysis reveals that simulated changes become statistically significant by mid-century in central areas and later in western regions. The results indicate a robust increase in wave height in the eastern North Sea and many regions of the Baltic Sea, as well as a consistent decrease in the western North Sea, particularly under high-emission scenarios. However, these trends are superimposed on substantial temporal and internal variability, highlighting the importance of using ensemble-based assessments to evaluate future marine climate conditions.
